A biweekly goth party at Chilkoot Charlie's has become wildly popular since launching five months ago.

It's 10 p.m. on a Tuesday at Chilkoot Charlie's, the Spenard nightclub, and as you walk deeper into the maze of rooms, the sound of heavy bass gets stronger.

Inside the Ice Bar, the dance floor is packed with people in black clothing adorned with chains and spikes, swaying to the hypnotic beats of darkwave music. On stage, two dancers flank the DJ and rev up the crowd with a vampy performance.

At 11 p.m., there's an announcement, and the crowd scatters to form a perimeter. They watch as Alex Edwards prepares to hang from the ceiling—using specialized piercing hooks placed under the skin of each of their knees.

As a metal cover of Britney Spears' "Toxic" plays over the speakers, ropes are attached to the hooks and Edwards is hoisted up by a pulley system until they're dangling upside down.

Welcome to Terrorwave—a once-every-two-weeks, queer-friendly goth party at Koot's that's become wildly popular since launching five months ago.

Edwards, a professional piercer at an Anchorage shop, is no stranger to the art of suspension.

Last summer, with a group of friends, they even pulled a car across C Street with hooks attached to their back. But the crowd at the mid-August Terrorwave took things to a new level.

"That was the most nervous I've been since my first time," Edwards said afterward. "I got my Spider-Man kiss with my partner, and that's really what helped me stay up there."

Edwards was just one of the attractions at Terrorwave: The recurring parties feature dancers, music, live performances, tarot readings, costume contests and more.

The events began in May, when 21-year-old Scarlet Redd approached Koot's management and pitched the idea.

Redd, who grew up in Anchorage, began experimenting with her personal style as a teenager. She credits her experience as a dancer with helping her embrace the goth aesthetic.

"That's when I realized I could be naked, creepy and scary," she said.

Redd's idea of a goth night event stemmed from a fetish-themed fundraiser she attended last spring at Mad Myrna's, the longtime gay club downtown—where she found a community interested in gothic music and culture that wanted more chances to meet up.

A series at Koot's seemed like a natural progression.

Since the 1970s, the club has been a staple in Anchorage nightlife and a hub for subculture, with events ranging from electronic dance parties to burlesque shows and everything in between.

As the idea of Terrorwave spread, Redd pulled together a group of friends, including Akira, a DJ who had recently moved to Anchorage, and they got to work.

"By June we had really exploded," said Redd. "It's really crazy. I never expected it to blow up the way it did, but I think it just was meant to work out."

Akira, who became Redd's Terrorwave partner in crime, first arrived in Alaska through the military, where they struggled to balance the required conformity with their personal style and individuality.

"It was a damn near impossible task," they said. "You have to look like everyone else. You have to act like everyone else. You have to talk like everyone else. You have to stand, shit, sit, and eat like everyone else."

They added: "It was only on the weekends where I could finally take off that mask and put on who I really am."

Both Akira and Redd wanted a space where everyone feels safe being their authentic selves, including queer people.

"I'm straight as hell but I was raised by women and drag queens," Akira said. "I grew up watching drag movies and going to drag shows. I'm very much into that culture."

Redd added: "If there's a problem, that person's escorted out immediately. We don't tolerate any kind of harassment or bullying."

The 30-year-old bartender has worked at Koot's for four years and, at each shift, proudly sports colorful makeup, elaborate eyeliner and striking outfits that could be straight out of a graphic novel.

For Bretey, who's worked at each Terrorwave event, the parties connected him to a new, hidden community in Anchorage.

Since the parties started, Bretey has noticed more and more new faces around the bar, even on nights when Terrorwave isn't happening—which he takes as a sign that people are finding a sense of community at Koot's.

"I didn't know any of these people existed at all," he said. "There's so many of them, and it's like, every week, more of them show up—and it's really cool."

For Bretey and many other Terrorwave participants he's talked to, the parties are creating connections people didn't know they were missing. He encourages people to see what the parties are about—even if they sound different, or if the name "Terrorwave" may not exactly project a warm or inviting vibe.

"I didn't feel accepted anywhere," Bretey said. "But it's nice to be embraced for what you are."

He added: "This is one of the few places that I found that I'll actually achieve that goal."

ANCHORAGE, Alaska – The Bureau of Land Management will provide the public an additional week to comment on a proposed 30-year right-of-way renewal for Alaska’s famed Trans Alaska Pipeline System. With this extension, the scoping period, which began on Aug. 26, 2026, now closes on Sept. 17, 2026.

This scoping period is a key step in the environmental review process analyzing the potential renewal of the 800-mile pipeline’s right-of-way. Earlier this summer, the BLM received a 30-year right-of-way renewal application from Alyeska Pipeline Service Company, which operates the pipeline on behalf of its three owner companies: Harvest Alaska, LLC; ConocoPhillips Transportation Alaska Inc.; and ExxonMobil Pipeline Company LLC.

The Trans Alaska Pipeline System was first authorized in 1974 and constructed between 1974 and 1977. Since construction, the pipeline has transported over 19 billion barrels of oil from Alaska’s North Slope to in-state refineries and the Valdez Marine Terminal. In 2025, the Trans Alaska Pipeline System transported an average of 463,000 barrels per day.

#54. As vote nears, Trump’s AI gamble fuels economic uncertainty

In September, just weeks before Americans were set to vote in this year’s midterm elections, a sudden panic over the dangers posed by artificial intelligence caught the White House off guard. A whistleblower had gained viral attention warning the technology could soon pose an existential threat.

President Trump was quick to dismiss concerns, evoking urgency in the U.S. race to achieve global dominance in superintelligence. He rejected calls to negotiate guardrails on the most advanced AI models with China’s leader, Xi Jinping, weeks later, during a prime opportunity to do so in Washington.

Privately, the president expressed concern that public hysteria over AI could jolt markets that have become excessively concentrated in AI-related stocks. He had already seen fresh polling that showed widespread bipartisan opposition to the boom in construction of AI data centers, a push by Trump that has cannibalized chips and energy from other sectors of the economy, driving up costs.

Now, on the campaign trail in the final days before the vote, Trump is vowing to expand a “golden age” of prosperity through the end of his presidency. But experts say his policies on AI have set the U.S. economy up for a period of tremendous risk over the next two years.

Some of the world’s leading financiers are sounding increasingly alarmed about an AI bubble. Ray Dalio, founder of Bridgewater Associates, has warned that AI stocks have become detached from financial fundamentals, pushing markets to precarious highs. Ken Griffin, the world’s richest hedge fund owner, said last week that AI is “almost certainly” fueling a market bubble that could be on the verge of a burst.

Economists warn that Trump has overseen fiscal policy that would give the federal government fewer tools than it has had in the past to respond to a crisis.

“Trump could easily run into a financial crisis before his term is up,” said Kenneth Rogoff, an economist at Harvard. “It is entirely plausible to have a 30% — or more — drop in the stock market, and in the context of an economy so dependent on AI, this in turn could easily lead to a recession and widespread financial distress.”

Spending on data center infrastructure last winter and spring accounted for nearly all U.S. economic growth, according to independent analyses. Without it, growth would have slowed to a mere fraction of a percentage point, leaving the economy nearly stagnant and flirting with recession.

The Nasdaq 100 has about 70% of its index weight in companies that depend on AI, from semiconductor manufacturers and Big Tech giants to cloud platforms and software providers. Anthropic, one of the top AI industry leaders, plans an initial public offering next month, while its chief rival, OpenAI, expects to go public next year.

“Because government finances are already strained, with the national debt on a patently unsustainable trajectory, there is no guarantee of a sharp drop in long-term borrowing rates for the government,” Rogoff continued. “So the options for bailing out the financial sector, or fighting recession, could be much more constrained than during the global financial crisis.”

While doubling down on the promise of an untested, frontier technology, Trump’s other economic policies may be hobbling its growth. Tariffs have raised the costs of imported chips, servers, networking equipment, metals and electrical components, and visa restrictions have tied the hands of industry leaders trying to secure the world’s best talent. Runaway federal deficits have driven long-term bond interest rates, adding pressure on AI firms already strapped with debt.

Servaas Storm, an economist closely following the AI sector at the Delft University of Technology, said the industry is already facing severe financial headwinds that could precipitate a panic.

Prices for energy, chips and minerals are driving up the cost of doing business, while early AI adopters such as Microsoft, Meta and Walmart are scaling back its use as employees spend too much time correcting errors, cleaning up low-quality output and addressing security lapses.

Slower adoption could further complicate the search for revenue, particularly for companies already struggling to monetize the technology. For now, hyperscalers are shouldering the cost of financing frontier AI labs that, as Storm put it, “do not have a credible path to profitability.”

“There is indeed an AI bubble in the stock market and in data center construction, which could pop rather soon, as rising debt loads and elevated interest rates could prove too much to sustain it,” Storm said. “That moment is not imminent, but it is approaching — and it will very likely happen before the end of Trump’s presidency.”

An economy already under duress from Trump’s other economic policies — from tariffs and tax cuts to the Iran war — could precipitate further pain, said Darrell Duffie, a finance professor at the Stanford Graduate School of Business.

Leading AI companies are already running “day-after” drills in anticipation of an AI-related crisis within the next year, according to Axios.

Americans head into election day deeply pessimistic about the economy, with 77% rating it fair or poor, according to a Fox News poll. A separate CNBC survey found that just 34% approve of Trump’s handling of the economy, while 35% of Republicans disapprove — a record high within his own party.

Inflation has fallen sharply from its 9% peak in 2022, during a pandemic-era surge, reaching 2.9% by the end of Biden’s presidency. Under Trump, it has climbed once again to 3.7%, driven by rising energy, transportation and housing costs — among the top concerns for voters entering next month’s vote.

Persistent inflation has forced the Federal Reserve to keep interest rates elevated, squeezing borrowers. And those rates — paired with high government spending and concerns over mounting deficits — have pressured the bond market, pushing 10-year Treasury yields to levels not seen since the early 2000s.

“Financial crises, by their nature, are virtually impossible to predict,” Duffie said, asked whether he shared concerns of a potential AI bubble.

“If we know we are going to be in a crisis soon,” he added, “markets advance the crisis to now.”

#55. Polls to open in Kipnuk for first time since ex-typhoon devastated Western Alaska village

Polls are set to open in the village of Kipnuk on Tuesday for the first time since the remnants of Typhoon Halong ravaged Western Alaska villages last fall.

Most residents of Kipnuk and nearby Kwigillingok were evacuated in the days after the storm amid widespread devastation. Polling places in the two villages never opened for the August primary.

And turnout, predictably, fell off a cliff, said Michelle Sparck with the nonpartisan group Get Out The Native Vote. Just a handful of residents were able to cast ballots, she said, a 90% drop from turnout two years earlier.

More than 500 people are registered to vote in Kipnuk and Kwigillingok. And while hundreds of the villages’ former residents are scattered across the state, including many in Anchorage, a significant number remain.

Estimates vary widely, but “there's between 150 and 250 people living in Kipnuk,” Sparck said. “Some of those are permanent residents that never wanted to leave in the first place, or they had salvageable housing, and then a large part of that is a workforce.”

So Sparck and other voting-access groups have been working with the Division of Elections to set up in-person absentee voting in the two communities.

The division declined an interview request, but Sparck said state officials have been receptive to designating the two villages as so-called “permanent absentee voting” sites. The Lower Kuskokwim School District agreed to allow Kipnuk’s Chief Paul Memorial School to be used as a polling place. Community members found a volunteer willing to staff the voting site.

And now, polls are set to open in Kipnuk. Starting Tuesday, Oct. 27, polls will open to voters for four hours each weekday, from 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., through Election Day, the Division of Elections announced Thursday.

Sparck said she and others are still pushing to open a polling site in Kwigillingok, where far fewer people remain. For the moment, she said, the only holdup was finding someone willing to manage the polls.

“All it takes is a willing person, and that person can make a huge difference for their village, for their community, for their region, for their culture, for their people, their languages,” Sparck said.

That’s true beyond the storm-damaged villages, she said. Across rural Alaska, unexpected challenges that threaten voters’ ability to cast ballots come up all the time, she said.

Residents throughout rural Alaska should reach out to Get Out The Native Vote or the Alaska Federation of Natives as they build a roster of people willing to pitch in at the polls, Sparck said.

#56. 2026 Yukon River Chinook Run Timing Forecast

The Yukon River Chinook Run Timing Project is a collaboration between the Alaska Department of Fish & Game, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, and the Alaska Ocean Observing System.

Yukon River Chinook are expected to arrive on the Yukon River Delta with average run timing in 2026: The first significant pulse (15% point) is expected by June 12th and 50% of the run is expected to arrive by June 20th. Note that the forecast model used this year is less powerful than the one used in previous years. This change is due to the discontinuation of sea surface temperature and sea ice data products used normally. See Project Status section for more detail and for information on future plans for the project.

Moderate air temperature at the Nome, AK airport during the month of April is generally associated with average run timing (Mundy & Evenson 2011)1. In 2026, April mean air temperature at the Nome, AK airport (AMATC) was -5.4°C, slightly warmer than the long-term average of -6.6°C (range -17.1°C to 1.3°C).

Figure 1: Scatterplot of median Yukon River Chinook run timing (MDJ) against April mean air temperature at the Nome, AK airport (AMATC). A solid vertical bar at -5.4°C marks the observed value for AMATC in 2026.

The forecast model is based on the relationship previously established in Mundy & Evenson (2011)1 which identified a set of three environmental variables that correlate with the run timing of Yukon River Chinook: April mean air temperature at the Nome, AK airport (1961–2026; AMATC), May mean sea surface temperature (1961–2025; MSSTC) in the nominal marine staging area just off the Yukon River Delta, and the Spring-time proportion of ice cover (1970–2025; PICE), also measured over the nominal marine staging area. The forecast model for 2026 is based only on AMATC due to data availability.

The forecast model is made of three independent sub-models, where each sub-model is a multiple linear regression with a response of either the 15th (FIFDJ), 25th (QDJ), or 50th (MDJ) percentile of cumulative catch-per-unit effort (CPUE) in either commercial or test fisheries located on the delta (1961–2023). Note the time series of CPUE data ends in 2023 due to changes in the operation of the Lower Yukon Test Fishery.

Based upon the historical relationship with CPUE in commercial and test fishery catches on the Yukon River Delta (1961–2023) and AMATC, the predicted dates of three percentiles of CPUE in the Lower Yukon Test Fishery are June 12th (15%), June 15th (25%), and June 20th (50%) (Table 1).

Table 1: Predicted dates (June 12th, 15th, 20th) of three percentiles (15%, 25%, 50%) of cumulative catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE) and the associated model formula for each.

We quantified the accuracy of the forecast sub-models with a hindcasting approach, using the most recent fifteen years of runs. For each sub-model, we calculated a set of prediction-oriented evaluation criteria (Table 2). All three sub-models (FIFDJ, QDJ, MDJ) perform similarly, with mean absolute prediction errors (MAPE) 3.87–4.40 days. We calculated prediction intervals by multiplying each model’s standard error by 2σ to get prediction interval widths ranging from 5.5–6.1 days which contained the observed run timing 40–53% of the time. Last, we calculated the maximum absolute residual to be 10–12 days and prediction bias to be -4.4 to -2.4 days. A negative value for bias means the model tends to forecast earlier than observed run timing. The forecast’s bias is an area for future forecast improvements.

Table 2: Model performance metrics for the three sub-models that make up the forecast. MAPE: Mean Absolute Prediction Error and its standard deviation. Bias is the mean residual. Max. Residual is the maximum absolute residual.

As mentioned above, this year’s forecast uses a reduced model due to the discontinuation of the data products associated with the MSSTC and PICE variables used in the full model. MSSTC is derived from NCEP-NCAR Reanalysis 1 which has been discontinued and replaced by CORe as of March, 2026. PICE is derived from Near-Real-Time DMSP SSMIS Daily Polar Gridded Sea Ice Concentrations, Version 1 which has been replaced by an AMSR2-based product as part of the National Snow and Ice Data Center’s replacement of its SSMIS-based products. Attempts were made to evaluate the replacement data products but initial results show more work is needed to integrate them into the forecast.

The loss of these two data products is significant but isn’t the only change affecting this project. Since 2024, the Lower Yukon Test Fishery hasn’t operated sites specifically for Chinook. This means the CPUE data series used in both the full and reduced models ends in 2023 and forecast models are no longer being fit to the most recent previous runs. These two changes indicate a new modeling approach is likely needed in order to produce a useful forecast product.

Some of Alaska’s most polarizing development projects — from oil drilling in the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge to a mining road in the Northwest Arctic — share a key feature.

They’re under the direction of the same state-owned corporation, one that’s been a lightning rod for diverging visions of Alaska’s economy and future.

In the past year, the board of the Alaska Industrial Development and Export Authority, or AIDEA, has approved some $250 million for Arctic oil exploration and two separate mining road proposals.

But only a fraction of that money has been spent. The projects haven’t been built.

And now, with Alaskans set to elect a new governor Nov. 3, AIDEA’s fate is in limbo.

If Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins, the current frontrunner, wins the race to succeed outgoing Republican Gov. Mike Dunleavy, the export authority could be poised for a sharp change in direction.

In a recent interview, Kreiss-Tomkins said he’s been “frustrated” by the authority, citing concerns about a lack of transparency and a sense that it’s “rogue and off on its own.”

If elected, Kreiss-Tomkins said, he would “completely reassess everything that AIDEA is looking at.”

Other issues — the Permanent Fund dividend, oil taxes, education — have dominated the discourse in this year’s governor’s race.

But political observers agree that the next governor’s approach to AIDEA could also have big implications for the state’s finances and nationally significant development proposals across Alaska.

Among the projects being led by AIDEA are the Ambler Road, a proposed 210-mile mining road that would run through northern Alaska and across a national preserve; the West Susitna Access project, a separate mining haul route through a remote stretch of the Susitna Valley, west of Anchorage; and oil development in the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge.

#59. New program aims to place dozens of homeless Anchorage families into market-rate apartments next year

This story has been made free to access as a public service.

Some 40 homeless families or individuals in Anchorage may find housing next year through a new voucher program being finalized by the municipal Health Department and the Alaska Housing Finance Corp., according to officials.

Both agencies are using federal funds from the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development, or HUD, to provide two-year vouchers to people deemed eligible for the program that’s dubbed “Homelessness to Housing.”

The total amount of funding available for the program is $581,000. Of that, $250,000 will come from AHFC through its Housing Choice Voucher program, commonly known as Section 8, the federal government’s primary vehicle for helping extremely low-income families, the disabled, veterans and the elderly find housing.

The municipality will steer $250,000 of its HUD funding toward the program as well as pay the Alaska Housing Finance Corp. $31,000 to cover administrative costs, according to the Health Department’s Frankie Dahl, an outreach coordinator who gave an overview at the Anchorage Assembly’s Health and Homelessness Committee meeting Wednesday.

Case managers who work at shelters or in outreach will compile lists of people who fit the program criteria, described as “homeless and ready for housing.” They’ll also help clients gather necessary documentation and complete an application.

One of the requirements for a voucher is that the household must continue to work with a case manager throughout the duration of their participation in the program, Dahl said.

“Case management is going to be a vital part of this,” Dahl said.

Anchorage Assembly member Yarrow Silvers, who chairs the Health and Homelessness Committee, said she likes that case management is a core feature of the program and that it’s focused on housing readiness.

“What makes this program different is that it focuses on housing people who are participating with outreach and shelter providers and who are ready for housing, rather than just pulling names off a list,” Silvers said by text.

Covenant House Alaska has used a similar model with a high success rate “in not just getting people housed but in them being able to maintain their housing over time,” Silvers said.

The municipality and AHFC started talking about joining forces on a voucher program for the first time earlier this year, and they expect to have details finalized before year’s end. The resulting contract would need Anchorage Assembly approval to move forward.

The committee expects the submission window for voucher applications to open in January.

Alaska Housing Finance Corp. administrators will determine who gets a voucher and it’ll likely be done on a first-come, first-served basis, said Thea Agnew Bemben, Mayor Suzanne LaFrance’s special assistant for homelessness and community safety.

Would-be tenants can search for housing on their own or work with case managers to find available units. Once a lease is signed, the tenant will be responsible for paying the landlord 30% of the monthly rent based on their adjusted gross income. AHFC will pay the rest, using the HUD funding.

Although Anchorage has an extremely tight housing market, some landlords like to rent to people with housing vouchers because they know the bulk of the payments are coming from a guaranteed funding source.

“We get new ones regularly,” said Carrie Collins, Alaska Housing Finance Corp. policy and program manager.

The housing agency worked closely with landlords during the pandemic and recruited quite a few through that engagement process and through an incentive program that has since expired, added Stacy Barnes, AHFC director of governmental relations and public affairs.

“We hear that what landlords really appreciate about this program is the consistency with which AHFC pays the rent every month,” Barnes said.

While the voucher program as currently designed is only for two years, Agnew Bemben said the goal is to keep it going longer through an ongoing partnership with AHFC.

“Our intention is that it continues on,” she said.

Both she and Dahl acknowledged that while getting 40 homeless families into housing is a worthy goal, it’s a long way from solving the city’s sizable problem with homelessness.

At last count, 291 people were living outdoors in Anchorage, according to the Point in Time count earlier this year. That’s one night in January where outreach workers nationally count the number of people they encounter sleeping outside.

Another 1,354 are living in shelters or transitional housing, according to the count. Hundreds of others are living in some other form of supported housing, while hundreds more are thought to be partially homeless, either couch-surfing, doubling up with relatives or friends, or living in a car or some other place not designed for human habitation.

All told, some 2,400 people were considered to be experiencing some form of homelessness in Anchorage, according to data compiled by the Anchorage Coalition to End Homelessness.

Given the size of the unhoused population, the new voucher program is a small but much-needed step in the right direction, according to advocates.

“This is just one tool in the toolbox,” Agnew Bemben said. “Forty vouchers is not going to solve the problem, but every time we do one of these things it creates another opportunity for people to move into housing and out of homelessness.”

Why should one woman’s apology determine whether an entire party deserves to win?

And why does the burden of demonstrating compassion so often land on women?

If this is a teachable moment, let’s examine who gets grace, who gets power, and who gets handed the sword to fall upon.

Former U.S. Rep. Mary Peltola has targeted economic concerns as a priority as she fights to unseat Alaska’s U.S. Sen. Dan Sullivan in one of the nation’s most important races.

#66. Why are Juneau’s highest-paid city employees mostly cops?

Juneau | Local Government | Public Safety

Juneau’s highest-paid city employee in 2025 was a cop. So was the second highest. And the third. In fact, 6 out of the 10 top-paid city employees last year were officers at the Juneau Police Department.

That’s according to a records request by KTOO to the City and Borough of Juneau. Last year, Juneau’s top earner was Police Sgt. Michael Wise. He made just over $272,000 in gross earnings. By comparison, the city’s top administrator — the city manager — earned about $230,000. The police chief, Derek Bos, earned $191,000. He wasn’t in the top 10.

Police leadership said the data underscores the department’s years-long struggle to fill vacant positions. Right now, the department is short 10 officers. To fill that gap, some officers have been clocking in more and more overtime. But that comes with its own trade-offs — the National Policing Institute warns that long shifts and extensive overtime can degrade an officer’s decision-making ability, creating safety risks for the community and the officers.

Juneau’s top paid city employees in terms of gross earnings in 2025 were Police Sergeants Michael Wise and Brian Dallas, and Police Officer Terry Allen, according to the data.

Wise ranked at the top with gross earnings of about $272,000 in 2025 — which breaks down to nearly $23,000 a month. Dallas made $260,000 and Allen made $230,000.

A lot of that is made up of overtime pay, which the police chief and city manager aren’t eligible for. In 2025, Wise, Dallas and Allen were paid a baseline rate of between $63 and $72 an hour, and worked between 629 and 1,050 hours of overtime. All three officers declined an interview with KTOO.

The amount an officer gets paid working overtime is determined by a formula in the police union’s contract with the city. Generally, it’s about 1.5 times their normal pay. Wise, Dallas and Allen are also longtime employees at the department, each with decades of service.

Officer Allen clocked the most overtime, topping out with more than 1,000 hours — which averages to roughly 20 extra hours every week beyond his regular job.

Despite police advocacy groups citing the potentially dangerous consequences of fatigue and long shifts, paying for overtime has become a growing piece of the Juneau Police Department’s budget. According to data, the department has spent more on overtime pay every year for at least the past five years. Between fiscal year 2021 and 2025, JPD spent a total of $6.5 million in overtime pay, which is roughly $2.3 million more than what was originally budgeted.

Despite that extra overtime pay, data shows the department’s spending as a whole each year was actually below budget. That’s largely because of its high number of staff vacancies — when positions go unfilled, the city saves money on those salaries.

It’s common to see police officers working large amounts of overtime and ranking at the top of their city’s pay charts. Larger cities in the U.S. like San Diego, San Francisco, and Boston also have officers pulling in six-figure incomes, most commonly because of overtime work due to short staffing. Like Juneau, those communities are also grappling with having enough officers on duty and the safety issues that could rise from overworking those officers.

In an interview with KTOO, Juneau Police Chief Derek Bos said overtime work has become essential for the department, especially in recent years.

“We’re in this catch-22 where we have to cover shifts, which means there has to be overtime that people work, but we still have to care for everybody who’s working that much,” he said. “Not showing up is not an option.”

But Bos contends that the department, which currently has 45 officers, doesn’t demand mandatory overtime, at least not right now. Instead, officers usually sign up voluntarily. And some more than others.

“There is nothing that says we expect them to work 90 hours or 91 hours or 93 and a half hours,” he said. “The expectation is that those shifts get covered, but there’s discretion on who signs up for those and how that works.”

Bos said short staffing for both officers and dispatchers has unfortunately become a norm at the department despite robust efforts to recruit new members. A few years ago, the department lowered the minimum number of officers required to be on patrol.

He said the department has implemented guardrails to reduce burnout and fatigue for officers, like mandatory rest periods and mental health support. If an officer works 16 hours or more, they are required to take at least an 8-hour break.

“We track how much they’re working, how much is too much, and sometimes that comes down to the individual. It comes down to what they’re doing,” he said.

He said the department is constantly trying to address burnout and fatigue. But it’s a balancing act with the responsibilities the department has to the community.

“We are there 365 days a year, 24 hours a day. It would not be okay for us to say we’re only going to work 16 hours a day, because then what happens the other eight hours of the day? So, not being there is not really an option,” he said.

Juneau Police Sgt. Sterling Salisbury is the chapter president for the Public Safety Employees Association, the union that represents the majority of JPD staff. In an interview with KTOO, he said the overtime data doesn’t surprise him, and he worries some officers are nearing their breaking point.

“You can only wring a dry rag so many times before the water stops coming out,” he said.

When asked about safety issues with overworked officers, Salisbury said staffing shortages can make policing much more stressful.

“When you’re at that minimum staffing or very close to minimum staffing, you’re pretty reactive,” he said. “You’re not super proactive, just because you’re constantly trying to play catch up.”

Salisbury said the union has tried for years to get a contract that makes Juneau’s department stand out among other agencies in Alaska. The union negotiates in the hopes that it will attract new people to fill prolonged vacancies.

“If you want to beat those competitors, you have to look more attractive — that’s really what it boils down to,” he said.

But Salisbury said it’s been difficult. This past contract negotiation cycle, it took a year for the union and city to negotiate a contract, and it required arbitration. While JPD pays entry-level offers more than many other departments in the state, Salisbury said it still doesn’t seem to be moving the needle.

Salisbury said offering Juneau officers more money isn’t the only solution to fixing the department’s chronic vacancies, but it definitely wouldn’t hurt.

“I always tell people, ‘Well, if you paid someone $500 an hour, we would be staffed next week. If you paid someone $5 an hour, we would have no staff.’ And so you have to find that middle ground of what can the city afford, what is needed and what’s realistic,” he said.

The city has its own money issues right now, says Mayor Beth Weldon.

“We’re not in a position that we can throw money at officers,” she said.

Last year, the Juneau Assembly voted to close one city facility, reduce funding to some services and dip into its reserves to patch a multimillion-dollar recurring budget hole. This coming budget cycle, it’s expected to face a similar reality.

Weldon said the city does its best to make its pay competitive with other agencies across the state. But she said they also have to be realistic with its budget restraints.

“There is no clean solution — wish there was — but there’s no clean solution,” she said. “All we can do is keep trying to be the best employer in the city.”

She said there are several factors that make Juneau a more difficult place to recruit employees in general, not just police. On top of that, recruiting and retaining officers is a nationwide problem. That is due in part to increased public attention on policing in the U.S. and changing workforce expectations.

“People just don’t want to be police officers like they used to,” Weldon said. “I mean, how many kids this day and age do you know that say they want to be a police officer when they grow up? Very few.”

#76. Opinion: Five things to know about Anchorage’s fiscal future

Let’s be real: The Municipality of Anchorage doesn’t have the resources it needs to deliver the services residents want and deserve. We are nearing a breaking point decades in the making, and it’s time to decide what we’re going to do about it.

Over the coming months, we’re asking you to join the conversation about our fiscal future. To do that, here are five things you should know about where we’ve come from and how our budget has changed.

The year is 1983. With 231,000 residents, Anchorage is growing rapidly. The trans-Alaska pipeline is flowing, and so is the money. The municipality’s budget is $194 million ($655 million in 2026 dollars), and 42% of it is funded by the state of Alaska.

Amid an explosion of population growth, capital investments and government spending, voters enact the tax cap.

Enshrined to this day in our municipal charter, the cap limits the municipality’s budget to no more than the previous year’s budget, adjusted for inflation and population change.

Today, Anchorage has 291,000 residents, a sprawling footprint to maintain, a $666 million municipal budget and only $1 million to $4 million from the state. Capped for the past 40 years, the Muni has cut staff, trimmed or eliminated services and chased funding to fill the gaps in our strained budget.

Over 60% of the municipal operating budget funds three departments: the Anchorage Police Department ($145.9 million), Anchorage Fire Department ($123.3 million) and Maintenance & Operations ($117.8 million).

Most of the budget is spent on labor — the people who work every day to run government, from fixing light poles to answering emergency calls. A meaningful property tax decrease will cut departments that keep our community safe and open for business.

When the city of Anchorage and Greater Anchorage Area Borough unified in 1975 to become the Municipality of Anchorage, a core merger dispute was resolved by a system that shapes our government to this day: service areas.

The basic concept is that certain geographic areas vote to fund certain public services. Voters in the Anchorage Roads & Drainage Service Area may approve bonds to improve streets, roads and sidewalks within the area. Smaller areas, like the Glen Alps Limited Road Service Area, approve their own mill rates to fund their own road maintenance.

The amount we all pay to fund the municipality’s general government — libraries, planning, administration, municipal prosecutors, etc. — is very small. A typical tax bill is mostly levying dollars that can only be used in a particular service area for the area’s functions: police, fire, parks, building safety or roads.

For 50 years, the service area concept has allowed communities to vote themselves into the level of government they want and retain a measure of local control. As a result, not everyone pays for the same services, and some people use services and amenities they don’t pay for.

Remember when we mentioned that 42% of the municipal budget in 1983 was funded by the state of Alaska? Its contribution helped build new things and maintain them too. But in 2026, that rate is down to 0.05%.

We have three big problems: 1) we have billions of dollars in backlog as the port, schools and municipal facilities, stormwater drainage, pipes and roads are all reaching the end of their lives; 2) we built a sprawling city without enough housing to sustain it; and 3) our current budget structure doesn’t have enough flexibility to address today’s reality. Each problem stands as a barrier to the next, but to solve one nearly solves all three.

Of the 346 U.S. cities with a population over 100,000, we rank No. 1 in geographic area and No. 75 in population. We have fewer people paying for more infrastructure, and that infrastructure is reaching the end of its life expectancy. We built a city we can’t afford to maintain, and now we have a lot of work to do with about a billion dollars we don’t have.

The math starts to change when we add more housing to the existing footprint of our infrastructure. Housing that fills in the gaps along our roads and utility lines increases the tax base that funds that infrastructure. Taxpayer density brings financial stability; more housing means more people pay their share in the long run.

But we don’t live in the long run. We live today.

Today, the cost of keeping our streets safe is bursting under the tax cap. Without further investment, the only option is less — of everything.

Today, the municipality is facing tough fiscal decisions, and we’re asking you: What level of service are you willing to fund through your local government?

#77. King salmon runs are crashing. In Kodiak, a pioneering conservation project aims to save one

October 22, 2026 by Katherine Irving, KMXT - Kodiak

The mountains had turned orange and yellow above Kodiak’s Pillar Creek hatchery as James ‘Hawk’ Turman waited at the hatchery gate on a September day.

“It smells a little bit right now, because you got a bunch of pink salmon out there dying in the river,” he said as he unlocked the gate. “I’m waiting for a bunch of rain to just wash them all down.”

Turman manages the Pillar Creek hatchery. Most of the time, it’s home to the Kodiak Regional Aquaculture Association’s sockeye and silver salmon operations. But something new is happening here. The aquaculture association is trying to rehabilitate a crashing king salmon run from the Karluk River, on the southwest side of the island.

Eggs collected from those wild salmon are now incubating at the hatchery. And although many support the project, some scientists worry it could do more harm than good.

The Karluk run is one of only two native king salmon runs on Kodiak Island, and it has deep cultural and historical significance for the Alutiiq people. But the run is in major trouble. Last year, only 93 kings returned to the Karluk River. The year before, it was just 76.

Across Alaska, king salmon populations have been returning in record low numbers. Turman said the Karluk project, which according to the aquaculture association is the first of its kind in the state, might be the start of something bigger.

“Our efforts are being used as kind of a test case for other stocks of concern around the state,” he said. “Someone had to finally kick the can off the bridge, and why not us?”

But he said the project’s first year has not gone off without a hitch. The first part of the plan required catching a certain number of wild kings. The challenging terrain and the Karluk’s fast current meant staff had to catch the kings using the same method as a sportfisherman: a rod, reel and lure.

Only 300 kings returned this year, and they were swimming among 1.5 million pinks — several times what scientists expected. That made catching the kings like finding a needle in a haystack.

And the kings they did catch were mostly males.

“For the purposes of genetic diversity, we do have a minimum target of 25 females for that project, but we were not able to pen those up,” Turman said.

Ultimately, Turman said they spawned the five females they had with four different males each, and sent milt from some of the males to a lab in Seattle to be frozen and preserved.

Hatcheries have become increasingly controversial over the years, as evidence mounts that hatchery fish can harm wild fish populations.

Conservation hatcheries have gotten some flak, too.

“A conservation hatchery, all well-intentioned, absolutely can come at a detrimental cost,” said Peter Westley, a fisheries ecologist at the University of Alaska Fairbanks.

Westley said hatcheries can’t perfectly replicate wild reproduction. Hatcheries spawn fish by mixing the eggs from a female with milt from one or more males, usually chosen at random.

“However we choose to do it, the choice that we make is inevitably different than the choice that the female would have made,” Westley said.

Westley said that over time, this has consequences for the genetic strength of the population.

In the Lower 48, the results from conservation hatcheries have been a mixed bag. Some research has found that conservation hatcheries helped salmon and steelhead populations survive tough times in Washington and Idaho. Other conservation hatcheries, Westley said, have been less successful.

Westley said that in Oregon’s Columbia River, hatchery fish have largely replaced the wild stocks.

“Efforts in places like the Columbia River, functionally have not worked,” he said. “What has worked to restore salmon populations is restoring habitat and restoring the root causes of the problems.”

Alaska’s Department of Fish and Game is one of the aquaculture association’s partners in the Karluk effort. Fish and Game Commissioner Doug Vincent-Lang said they are doing everything they can to avoid an outcome like the Columbia River’s.

“We have so few kings coming back right now, we have to make sure that we’re not causing long-term genetic implications,” he said.

And with the dire state of the Karluk run, the choice may be between allowing the introduction of hatchery-raised fish, or ending up with no fish at all.

Stacey Imaq Simmons is a vice president of the Koniag Native Corporation, another partner in the project. She said the recent low king returns mean “doing nothing is no longer the low-risk option.”

Our Karluk kings have sustained our Sugpiaq Alutiiq people for more than 7,800 years,” she said. “So this isn’t just about restoring the fisheries. It’s about protecting a resource tied to subsistence, our culture, food security, and really the relationship our people have had with that watershed for thousands of years.”

Back at the hatchery, Turman said they’re still waiting on decisions from Fish and Game about what to do next. Vincent-Lang has voiced concerns that releasing so many fish from just a few parents could compromise the genetic diversity of the wild run.

But they have time. The baby kings likely wouldn’t go back into the river until at least 2028. If they do release them, the project’s managers would have several more years to decide whether to let wild fish spawn with hatchery fish, if any make it back to the river.

In the meantime, Turman said he’ll keep raising the kings, the same way he’s raised generations of salmon before them.

In one of the hatchery’s rooms, kept dark to mimic the lightlessness of a riverbed, he pulled out a tray of eggs taken from the Karluk kings.

“If you look closely at the eyes, you can see the shiny iris,” he said. “That’s the sub-stage we call golden eyes.”

#78. Alaskans to vote on language change in voter eligibility law in midterm election

This midterm election, Alaskan voters will decide whether to change state voter qualification language related to the citizenship requirement to vote.

Only U.S. citizens are eligible to vote in national and state elections. Ballot Measure 3, which appears on the ballot as 25USCV, would not change any requirements to vote, but proposes adding the word “only” to state statute.

In Alaska, the law states “a person may vote at any election who is a citizen of the United States.” Ballot Measure 3 asks voters whether to change language to add the word to read: “Only a person who is a citizen of the United States…may vote in any election.”

An eligible voter must also be at least 18 years of age, reside in the state and house district in which they seek to vote for at least 30 days just before the election, have registered to vote as required by law and not be registered to vote in another jurisdiction. Those eligibility requirements would remain unchanged.

The ballot measure was sponsored by several former Republican state legislators, including former state senators John Coghill of Fairbanks and Josh Revak of Anchorage, and former House Speaker Mike Chenault of Nikiski, who currently serves on the board of the Alaska Gaseline Development Corp.

Supporters of the measure, called Alaskans for Citizen Voting, argue the current law is unclear and should be updated to clarify only U.S. citizens may vote. The group has raised nearly $900,000 this election cycle, with more than $800,000 of that amount financed by major GOP donor and billionaire Richard Uihlein, according to reporting by the Anchorage Daily News.

The Alaska Division of Elections held two public hearings on the proposed ballot measure in July and August. Coghill testified at a Jul. 27 hearing in support.

“The current law says citizens may vote, but it does not specifically say that only citizens may vote, and this measure closes that gap, and it makes the law in Alaska unmistakably clear,” he said.

Coghill cited a proposal considered in Los Angeles, California earlier this year to allow noncitizens who have some form of legal permanent status — like Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals, Temporary Protected Status holders or green card holders — to vote in city and school board elections. The city council rescinded the ballot measure in June.

“This shows the issue is not hypothetical,” Coghill said. “So we should not wait for a similar fight here before clarifying our own law. The point of 25USCV is to close that door before it gets pushed open.”

The Alaska chapter is part of a national group, Americans for Citizen Voting, which works to pass citizen-only initiatives across the country. Five other states including Arizona, Arkansas, Kansas, South Dakota and West Virginia have proposed constitutional amendments this election to bar noncitizen voting.

Some elections watchdogs say the initiatives are a political stunt to turn conservative voters out at the polls this November, according to reporting by Stateline, particularly where Republicans are fighting for re-election and to maintain majority control in the U.S. House and Senate.

In Alaska, no group officially formed to oppose Ballot Measure 3, but several current legislators gave personal testimony in opposition at the July hearing.

Anchorage independent Rep. Alyse Galvin testified that Alaska already has a citizenship requirement, and the change would be redundant. She also pointed to increased scrutiny by the Alaska Division of Elections, which preemptively removed over 3,000 voters from the active voter list pending proof of citizenship and said the act instilled fear in her constituents with newly acquired U.S. citizenship in voting.

“They’re afraid to go into the polls. That’s not okay. They’re afraid because of all of this rhetoric around what’s happening with ICE and everything else. They don’t know if now they’re on some special list. How do I get off the list? All of that. They don’t know how to navigate the system in a way that makes them feel comfortable enough to vote,” Galvin said. “And frankly, I don’t know how to help them do that. That is a concern of mine, a deep concern of mine.”

“Our system somehow has created fear,” she continued. “And my worry is that this particular ballot measure is going to engender more of that, more of this sort of action, and more of this sort of fear.”

Anchorage Democratic Rep. Andrew Gray, who chairs the House Judiciary Committee, echoed the criticism that the ballot measure is unnecessary, and spoke to a recent case where he said U.S. nationals were prosecuted for voting in Alaska.

“I am not concerned about California. In Alaska, non-U.S. citizens are already forbidden from voting in local and state elections. This is what led to the arrest of voters in Whittier a couple of years ago,” he said.

Gray said the recent criminal prosecution of 11 American Samoan residents in Whittier — where some voted and a resident ran for school board — was a failure of the Division of Elections, law enforcement and those residents in understanding the law.

American Samoans are U.S. nationals, and have irrevocable rights to reside in the United States, but are not U.S. citizens and therefore do not have the right to vote in state or federal elections. In the Whittier case, 11 residents were charged with illegally voting, and the state dropped the charges after a court of appeals dismissed the case last month.

Gray said he opposes the ballot measure because it could eliminate the possibility of the Legislature or local jurisdictions changing the law to include U.S. nationals — like American Samoans — in the right to vote in Alaska elections.

“They should be able to vote. They should be able to run for local office like the school board, and they should be able to serve their local communities,” Gray said.

“There is room at this particular moment without this ballot measure of expanding being able to vote and serve in the local school board, for example, if we were to expand it to American nationals in Alaska,” he said. “I think that’s the just thing to do. I think it’s the right thing to do. I think it’s the moral thing to do.”

A change to voter eligibility to include U.S. nationals would require a change in state law passed by the Alaska State Legislature and signed by the governor to allow voting in state and local elections. A change to the law barring U.S. nationals from federal elections would require action from the U.S. Congress.

This midterm election, Alaskan voters will decide whether to change state voter qualification language related to the citizenship requirement to vote.

Only U.S. citizens are eligible to vote in national and state elections. Ballot Measure 3, which appears on the ballot as 25USCV, would not change any requirements to vote, but proposes adding the word “only” to state statute.

In Alaska, the law states “a person may vote at any election who is a citizen of the United States.” Ballot Measure 3 asks voters whether to change language to add the word to read: “Only a person who is a citizen of the United States…may vote in any election.”

An eligible voter must also be at least 18 years of age, reside in the state and house district in which they seek to vote for at least 30 days just before the election, have registered to vote as required by law and not be registered to vote in another jurisdiction. Those eligibility requirements would remain unchanged.

The ballot measure was sponsored by several former Republican state legislators, including former state senators John Coghill of Fairbanks and Josh Revak of Anchorage, and former House Speaker Mike Chenault of Nikiski, who currently serves on the board of the Alaska Gaseline Development Corp.

Supporters of the measure, called Alaskans for Citizen Voting, argue the current law is unclear and should be updated to clarify only U.S. citizens may vote. The group has raised nearly $900,000 this election cycle, with more than $800,000 of that amount financed by major GOP donor and billionaire Richard Uihlein, according to reporting by the Anchorage Daily News.

The Alaska Division of Elections held two public hearings on the proposed ballot measure in July and August. Coghill testified at a Jul. 27 hearing in support.

“The current law says citizens may vote, but it does not specifically say that only citizens may vote, and this measure closes that gap, and it makes the law in Alaska unmistakably clear,” he said.

Coghill cited a proposal considered in Los Angeles, California earlier this year to allow noncitizens who have some form of legal permanent status — like Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals, Temporary Protected Status holders or green card holders — to vote in city and school board elections. The city council rescinded the ballot measure in June.

“This shows the issue is not hypothetical,” Coghill said. “So we should not wait for a similar fight here before clarifying our own law. The point of 25USCV is to close that door before it gets pushed open.”

#86. King salmon runs are crashing. In Kodiak, a pioneering conservation project aims to save one.

The mountains had turned orange and yellow above Kodiak's Pillar Creek hatchery as James 'Hawk' Turman waited at the hatchery gate on a September day.

"It smells a little bit right now, because you got a bunch of pink salmon out there dying in the river," he said as he unlocked the gate. "I'm waiting for a bunch of rain to just wash them all down."

Turman manages the Pillar Creek hatchery. Most of the time, it's home to the Kodiak Regional Aquaculture Association's sockeye and silver salmon operations. But something new is happening here. The aquaculture association is trying to rehabilitate a crashing king salmon run from the Karluk River, on the southwest side of the island.

Eggs collected from those wild salmon are now incubating at the hatchery. And although many support the project, some scientists worry it could do more harm than good.

The Karluk run is one of only two native king salmon runs on Kodiak Island, and it has deep cultural and historical significance for the Alutiiq people. But the run is in major trouble. Last year, only 93 kings returned to the Karluk River. The year before, it was just 76.

Across Alaska, king salmon populations have been returning in record low numbers. Turman said the Karluk project, which according to the aquaculture association is the first of its kind in the state, might be the start of something bigger.

"Our efforts are being used as kind of a test case for other stocks of concern around the state," he said. "Someone had to finally kick the can off the bridge, and why not us?"

But he said the project's first year has not gone off without a hitch. The first part of the plan required catching a certain number of wild kings. The challenging terrain and the Karluk's fast current meant staff had to catch the kings using the same method as a sportfisherman: a rod, reel and lure.

Only 300 kings returned this year, and they were swimming among 1.5 million pinks — several times what scientists expected. That made catching the kings like finding a needle in a haystack.

And the kings they did catch were mostly males.

"For the purposes of genetic diversity, we do have a minimum target of 25 females for that project, but we were not able to pen those up," Turman said.

Ultimately, Turman said they spawned the five females they had with four different males each, and sent milt from some of the males to a lab in Seattle to be frozen and preserved.

Hatcheries have become increasingly controversial over the years, as evidence mounts that hatchery fish can harm wild fish populations.

Conservation hatcheries have gotten some flak, too.

"A conservation hatchery, all well-intentioned, absolutely can come at a detrimental cost," said Peter Westley, a fisheries ecologist at the University of Alaska Fairbanks.

Westley said hatcheries can't perfectly replicate wild reproduction. Hatcheries spawn fish by mixing the eggs from a female with milt from one or more males, usually chosen at random.

"However we choose to do it, the choice that we make is inevitably different than the choice that the female would have made," Westley said.

Westley said that over time, this has consequences for the genetic strength of the population.

In the Lower 48, the results from conservation hatcheries have been a mixed bag. Some research has found that conservation hatcheries helped salmon and steelhead populations survive tough times in Washington and Idaho. Other conservation hatcheries, Westley said, have been less successful.

Westley said that in Oregon's Columbia River, hatchery fish have largely replaced the wild stocks.

"Efforts in places like the Columbia River, functionally have not worked," he said. "What has worked to restore salmon populations is restoring habitat and restoring the root causes of the problems."

Alaska's Department of Fish and Game is one of the aquaculture association's partners in the Karluk effort. Fish and Game Commissioner Doug Vincent-Lang said they are doing everything they can to avoid an outcome like the Columbia River's.

"We have so few kings coming back right now, we have to make sure that we're not causing long-term genetic implications," he said.

And with the dire state of the Karluk run, the choice may be between allowing the introduction of hatchery-raised fish, or ending up with no fish at all.

Stacey Imaq Simmons is a vice president of the Koniag Native Corporation, another partner in the project. She said the recent low king returns mean "doing nothing is no longer the low-risk option."

Our Karluk kings have sustained our Sugpiaq Alutiiq people for more than 7,800 years," she said. "So this isn't just about restoring the fisheries. It's about protecting a resource tied to subsistence, our culture, food security, and really the relationship our people have had with that watershed for thousands of years."

Back at the hatchery, Turman said they're still waiting on decisions from Fish and Game about what to do next. Vincent-Lang has voiced concerns that releasing so many fish from just a few parents could compromise the genetic diversity of the wild run.

But they have time. The baby kings likely wouldn't go back into the river until at least 2028. If they do release them, the project's managers would have several more years to decide whether to let wild fish spawn with hatchery fish, if any make it back to the river.

In the meantime, Turman said he'll keep raising the kings, the same way he's raised generations of salmon before them.

In one of the hatchery's rooms, kept dark to mimic the lightlessness of a riverbed, he pulled out a tray of eggs taken from the Karluk kings.

"If you look closely at the eyes, you can see the shiny iris," he said. "That's the sub-stage we call golden eyes."

#100. 'She's our blessing': A family’s journey after ex-Typhoon Halong — and the doll that came with them

Lacey and Darrell John are among the hundreds of people from the Western Alaska coast forced to move to Anchorage after a storm spawned by Typhoon Halong nearly wiped out their village.

A year later, they still don't know if they'll ever move back to Kipnuk. They want to go home but don't know if it's even possible to return or rebuild, or whether doing so would be the right choice. Another fall storm could wash it all away again.

On Oct. 16 last year, they boarded a giant military transport plane for Anchorage. Inside a cargo bay as vast as a whale's belly, the Johns and other families driven out by the storm sat on the floor. Shoulder to shoulder, they filled the entire plane.

Since then, they've done their best to build a new life in Anchorage without letting go of the old one.

One of their first priorities was to enroll their daughter, Emi Grace, in a new school. They chose College Gate Elementary School, which was in the process of absorbing more than 70 new students uprooted by the storm.

The first day brought an immediate scare. The school bus mistakenly dropped off their 5-year-old daughter at the wrong shelter. The Johns worried, because she had never ridden a bus before. She also has a heart condition that has left her with a vulnerable immune system.

But once the mix-up was cleared up, the Johns felt College Gate was the right place for Emi. It was one of the best-suited schools in Anchorage to welcome children from Western Alaska. It has a Yup'ik language program, in which children are taught in both Yugtun and English. The Johns were grateful for that, because amid all they had lost, they hoped to keep the one thing the storm couldn't take from them: their language.

"Quyana caknek," or thank you, Lacey John said. "Yup'ik is our first language and now her language, and it is very strong in my family."

Emi is now 6 and returned to College Gate as a first grader this fall. Her kindergarten teacher, Pauline Bayayok, said Emi arrived already fluent Yup'ik, a rarity among the school's students from the city. Emi quickly became one of Bayayok's helpers, because she set an example without even realizing it.

"It helped the other kids try harder to learn Yup'ik, because they'd see us communicating in Yup'ik, and she would understand what we were talking about," Bayayok said.

The school also helped Emi adjust to her new life in Anchorage. Her parents said she adapted better than they did, partly because she did not fully understand why they had to move. But one day, she discovered the truth.

"I had pictures in my phone that I had taken before we left, but I didn't show her," Lacey said. "I didn't know she was looking through my phone, until she started crying when she saw fallen buildings."

Emi's father, Darrell, said they did their best to shield Emi from their worries.

"That part scares me, thinking about the future, not being able to go home, or go to a home, where there is no home," he said.

So much had changed overnight, but for Emi, one thing remained constant. Although the family left almost everything behind, Emi carried one small reminder of home: a Cabbage Patch doll she calls "Baby."

Wherever Emi went, Baby went too. Along the way, Lacey took photo after photo of Baby's journey. What started as pictures of a beloved doll became snapshots of the family's own journey.

From the very beginning, Emi was determined to take good care of her doll. On the evening of Oct. 11, hours before the village felt the full force of the storm, Emi asked her dad if they could throw a party for Baby.

"She was telling me it's her baby's birthday, and she wanted to make a cake, so we made a cake for her," Darrell said. "It was a strawberry I think."

So they turned out the lights, lit the candles and sang Happy Birthday to Baby in Yugtun.

In the early morning hours, the water rose rapidly and they barely had time to grab Emi and make it to the safety of the school. Even inside, the wind only got louder. They watched from a window and saw the lights go out in the village. Somehow, there was still cellphone service. Lacey was terrified by what others shared in texts and on Facebook.

"People were saying that they were floating away and needing help," Lacey said.

One family, swept away in the powerful current, reported that their house had been carried across the river.

"I was like kind of feeling guilty, not being able to do anything or send for help for the ones who were drifting away," she said.

By morning, their house ended up several miles from the village, stranded in the middle of the tundra, a jumbled and muddy mess. But there were surprises.

Shiney, the family's puppy, was waiting to be rescued. He was left behind, because the school shelter didn't allow pets. Instead, they put him in his kennel and set it on a table on the back porch. They expected the high water, but not for their house to be swept away. After the storm, the family thought they would never see their dog again. Somehow, Shiney escaped — and inside the battered house on the kitchen counter — sat Baby's birthday cake, completely intact.   Still, Darrell and Lacey realized they were lucky to ride out the storm in the school. The photos show how Emi watched over Baby during each move that followed.

First came the little bed she made on a gym mat at the Kipnuk school. Then there was the cot at the Egan Center downtown, one of the main shelters for evacuees. There, Baby was tucked in for the night with her sippy cup. After they moved into a hotel, Baby could be found napping on a pillow next to two new doll friends. Finally, when the Johns were able to move into a house and get settled in by January, Baby got a bunk bed of her own.

"I just want to say, Quyana, and I thank God for continuing to hold us," Lacey said. "No matter what we go through, he's always there, providing — all of the donations and the gifts we've been receiving, even up to this day."

While the kindness of others has eased their loss, Darrell has missed the daily routines of hunting and gathering.

Whether it was setting blackfish traps or collecting driftwood, each day had a rhythm. The flood took more than buildings and belongings. It also took a way of life.

Emi's older brother Joe struggled with the change. He has developmental disabilities and missed doing chores around the village. They gave him a sense of purpose and belonging. For Darrell, his very identity as a man was at stake.

"All men in Kipnuk are hunters," Darrell said. "They hunt to eat, to provide for their family."

Darrell also needed to find work. For the first time in his life, he had to look for a job among strangers.

"We need to get a resume. I never had a resume in my entire life. Because when we apply for a job, we know everybody," Darrell said. "We know what people do."

What was once a closely knit extended family is now scattered across the state. Lacey's mother is in Bethel. Their oldest son, Russell, went back to Kipnuk to help with the cleanup. And this fall, Lacey left her family in Anchorage and took a job as a school secretary in Bethel, the same position she held in Kipnuk. With retirement approaching, she didn't want to lose the benefits she had worked for, and they needed the income to afford their new life in Anchorage.

Yet even far from home, Baby's photos reveal moments of joy and normalcy, from a trip to the Dimond Center ice rink to the Seward Mermaid Festival, where a woman selling homemade hats gifted Baby with one. Other photos show Baby on a fishing trip to the Kenai. She even has her own mini carseat now, and Emi always insists on strapping her in.

"There's a saying that our children are watching us, and they're learning from us," Lacey said. "And seeing her take care of her doll shows us that we're taking good care of each other."

Two years earlier, someone in their family had given Baby to Emi for Christmas. Until the storm, the doll never left the house. But as she left their home for the last time that it still stood intact, Lacey tucked Baby into her bag, and Baby's journey began.

"I'm glad she had something to hold," Lacey said.

In the photos, Baby and Emi are always the focus. But outside the frame, there's the family.

"She's our gem. She's our gift. She's our blessing," Lacey said. "Seeing her happy makes us happy and keeps us going." Copyright 2026 KNBA
