The Bureau of Land Management (BLM) Alaska's Anchorage Field Office will temporarily close short segments of the Speedway, Viewpoint, and Moose Meadow trails this week on the Campbell Tract Special Recreation Management Area from to protect public safety while fuel treatment work is underway. The temporary closures last through July 25 and are necessary to allow crews to safely complete vegetation management activities – clearing fuel breaks – to reduce wildfire risk within Campbell Tract.

These fuel breaks were created over 20 years ago through a cooperative effort between the BLM and the Municipality of Anchorage, as well as other partners, to help protect Campbell Tract.

Visitors should obey all posted closure signs and encouraged to use alternate routes during the project. Trail access outside the affected segments will remain open where it is safe to do so.

The BLM appreciates the public's patience and cooperation while this important work is completed. These fuel reduction efforts help protect nearby communities, recreation opportunities, and natural resources by reducing hazardous vegetation and improving the area's resilience to wildfire.

A big breakup leads to a fling with a friend—one week later.

You wouldn’t know it from reading the paper or listening to the radio, but there’s a lot of sex in Anchorage.

Welcome to Alaska’s newest (and only?) love, sex and dating advice column. Our very first installment is below.

But first: Secretly have a crush on your ski buddy? Worried about bumping into an ex at Myrna's? Curious about opening your relationship, exploring a kink or finally telling your partner what you actually want in bed?

Ask us for help! In each issue, three experts — or, rather, two experts and one normie — will respond to a reader-submitted question.

You can submit questions at anchoragepress.com. Nothing is too juicy. We'll keep you anonymous. Just, for God’s sake, tell the truth. No more mystery troll notes from “(a)gape,” please.

Our expert panelists are M. K. Thekkumkattil and Amber Batts.

M.K. is a trans, disabled, kinky writer and nurse whose work can be found in The Sexuality of Care: On Nursing, Kink, and a Future Without Hospitals, among other publications.

Amber is an Alaska writer, rebel journalist, sex worker and advocate who has spent years writing about the things polite company tends to avoid — sex, violence, power, criminal justice.

Max Graham, a journalist who covers environmental issues, will offer a third perspective as a token straight white guy. He’s totally unqualified for this gig, but excited to keep an open mind…and to learn some new terms from Urban Dictionary.

This summer I’ve found myself on the precipice of romantic change. My previous boyfriend ended our nearly three-year relationship, and I was devastated (at first). However, a week later, I found myself exploring a new connection with a close mutual friend who had been offering solid emotional support and friendship. We have been friends for five years! I didn’t see this one coming.

Is this allowed? It feels like this should not be allowed within a week or two of my breakup. I also feel it’s worth adding that I initiated this new dynamic, and it was well received.

I’m worried that the timeline will be leaked and my previous boyfriend will find out. And also: What would our mutual friends say or think?

Things are further complicated because I’m leaving for a seasonal job to a very far away place, with little to no reliable communication for the next six months, and I’m not sure there’s enough time to explore this new connection further.

Yes, this is allowed! All kinds of chaotic and delightful and absurd human behavior is allowed! Congrats on your new connection!

First of all, have fun while you can. Exploring romantic and sexual connection in the safe container of long-term friendship can be an incredible way to expand what relationships feel like. Relationships do not have to follow a linear structure, or what poly people often refer to as the “relationship escalator.” They can transform during periods of increased availability – like, say, after ending a long-term relationship – and they can de-escalate when life circumstances don’t allow the kinds of communication and commitment that ongoing intimacy requires.

That said, it sounds like there’s a potent combo of breakup energy and new relationship energy (NRE) at play. Breakups are vulnerable! Our whole lives can get shaken up, and it’s common to put extra emotional weight on things that would be better if we simply didn’t take them so seriously. The attention, excitement, and — I hope — hot new sex (however you define sex) of NRE can be addictive and short-lived. We can confuse dopamine hits for compatibility.

I always advocate for slowing things down during the early phase of relationships. Don’t make promises you can’t keep! Trust people’s actions more than their words. You’ll find out over time whether you both have capacity, interest and compatibility with each other. Stay present and enjoy the time you have. You don’t need to know yet whether you’ll still be interested down the line.

Finally, I hope you've told at least one person about this. As a scorpio, I love protecting my most intimate connections from outside judgment and interference. But, secrets can be a breeding ground for shitty relationship dynamics. Who do you trust enough to share all your mess with? You need at least one person.

There is no Alaska Department of Breakups requiring a 30-day waiting period before you're given a permit to develop feelings for somebody else. Your relationship ended. You are single.

Sure, your ex may find out. And yeah, they may be hurt. Your mutual friends may have opinions. That is Anchorage being Anchorage. Let’s operate under the assumption that everyone in your circle will eventually know. But someone else's feelings about your choices aren't evidence that you've made a bad choice. You cannot control what they're going to think.

Now, let’s move on to something more interesting: what's happening between you and this friend.

You've known each other for five years, and they supported you through this breakup. Now, there's chemistry. That's not automatically a rebound; sometimes the plot twists because the characters finally notice each other. Who are we to question the timing of things?

But I do have a question I think is worth asking yourself: Are you genuinely excited about this person, or are you afraid of suddenly being alone?

Those two things can be true at the same time. After more than two years with someone, being suddenly single can leave a big hole in your life. Just make sure you're exploring this new connection because you want to know what's there, not because you're in a hurry to fill the space.

Which brings us to the six-month absence.

You don't have to decide whether this is your next great love before you leave. In fact, please don't. Talk to each other. Be clear about expectations.

Are you exploring something without promises?

Do you want to reconnect when you return?

Those questions matter a hell of a lot more than whether you're moving too fast.

Maybe this is a rebound. Maybe it's the beginning of something wonderful. Maybe you make out for a while and six months from now you're friends again. None of those outcomes makes this a mistake.

My vote: Plot twist. Proceed with curiosity, leave the guilt behind and let everyone else worry about their own love lives.

Oof, sorry about the breakup — but yay for new feelings!

I’d try to focus less on whether the vibe with your friend is “allowed” or whether the timeline will be “leaked,” and more on what you think you need to thrive.

Forget about your ex for a second. He’s not in charge of your love life. Neither are your mutual friends. It’s up to you, not them, to decide whether your new connection is the right thing for you.

How does the vibe with your friend feel? Are you really into him? Or does some part of you sense that this is about filling a post-breakup void?

Be brutally honest with yourself. A week is barely any time. Processing the end of a long-term relationship can take a while. And, in the meantime, if what you’re really looking for is a low-stakes rebound, you run the risk of leading your friend on if he’s looking for something more.

That said, if the new dynamic feels healthy and good — and not just a potentially messy attempt to replace your ex — there’s nothing wrong with it! In that case, lean in. Talk to each other about how you’re feeling. See where it goes.

And yes, being away for six months might complicate things. But could the time apart actually be a good thing? Could it give you more time to process your breakup and focus on your new single self? And could that time maybe even help you develop a stronger connection with your friend when you’re back, if that’s what you want?

You don’t need all the answers right now. Just be ruthlessly honest — both with yourself and with your friend — and the answers will come soon enough.

#103. Brazil votes in a tense Lula vs. Bolsonaro runoff with U.S. influence over Latin America at stake

RIO DE JANEIRO — Brazilians were voting in Sunday’s polarizing presidential runoff between incumbent President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and Flávio Bolsonaro, a contest that will determine whether the U.S. will significantly expand its growing influence in Latin America.

Polls showed the two candidates, who need more than 50% of the vote to become Brazil’s next leader in January, in a neck-and-neck race. The election pits the left-of-center veteran politician Lula against Bolsonaro, an ally of U.S. President Donald Trump and the son of imprisoned former President Jair Bolsonaro.

Flávio Bolsonaro bested Lula by more than 2.2 million votes in the first round of the election earlier this month. However, more than 33 million voters out of nearly 160 million did not turn out.

Bolsonaro and Lula have opposing ideological views on issues ranging from U.S.-Brazil relations and climate change to social welfare.

A win for Bolsonaro would signify a pivot to the right by Latin America’s most populous country and its largest economy, in a year when Trump allies have scored victories in Chile, Peru and Colombia. A victory for Lula would leave Brazil and Mexico with left-of-center leaders.

In many ways, Brazil’s runoff vote is another tight rematch between the country’s two polarized political forces that trigger passion as much as loathing. Four years ago, Lula beat Bolsonaro’s father by the narrowest margin since the country’s return to democracy in 1985.

Lula, who first governed Brazil between 2003 and 2010, has faced a tough reelection battle because of concerns about the cost of living and security. Bolsonaro made a tough-on-crime approach the cornerstone of his campaign and pledged to bring down food costs.

Both have been hit by corruption suspicions involving family members and allies, with Bolsonaro implicated in a sprawling scandal rocking Brazil. Lula previously spent more than a year in prison on corruption convictions that were eventually annulled.

Bolsonaro’s admission that he accepted about $12 million from disgraced former banker Daniel Vorcaro to produce a film on his father’s life has cast a shadow over his campaign. He is under investigation for corruption, money laundering and tax evasion in connection with the case.

As Bolsonaro voted at a school in Rio de Janeiro, his supporters shouted “Lula, thief, your place is in prison” while Lula’s supporters chanted in his support.

“The time of real change has arrived,” Bolsonaro said. “Brazil can’t go on another four years on its current trajectory.”

In the lead-up to the vote, Lula frequently portrayed Bolsonaro, whose father is under house arrest for attempting a coup, as a threat to democracy. Bolsonaro has promised to seek an amnesty for his father if elected.

Lula, trying to lure undecided voters, has invested in costly incentives. His government endorsed a debt relief program for small businesses and promised free public transportation on weekends. A major win then came when the Senate backed a popular proposal to end the six-day working week.

Lula thanked his voters for an outpouring of support while casting ballot in the city of Sao Bernardo do Campo, where he began his political career as a union leader in the 1960s. “It is a lot of generosity, a lot of care, a lot of love. I will be forever grateful,” Lula said.

Lula voters turned out Sunday driven by his history of supporting marginalized people and working to lift Brazilians out of poverty.

“Lula is a man of the people and he knows what it’s like to be poor,” Luzia da Souza, who runs a bar in Rio de Janeiro, said. “I find it impossible not to vote for him.”

Anderson Falcão, a 43-year-old journalist in Brasilia who sported a red shirt in support of Lula, said he worried about Bolsonaro’s long-term political vision, which he called “exclusionary.”

“It’s a project that harms the less fortunate, harms the worker, women, the forest, the Amazon,” he said.

Bolsonaro voters, many who wore yellow and green Brazil jerseys in support of the senator, said they want change.

“Things are really expensive and taxes are too high,” Fabianne Borges Santoro, a 45-year-old physiotherapist in Rio de Janeiro, said. She added that she once voted for Lula’s party but now associates it with corruption.

Davi Vidal de Negreiros Nobrega Leite Andrade, a 20-year-old computer science student in Brasilia, said he prefers Bolsonaro’s economic plans over what Lula has done.

Kelvin Gomes Pimental, a 28-year-old data analyst in Brasilia, voted for a third-party candidate in the first round, making his vote a decisive one in this election. He ultimately decided to vote for Lula.

“It’s not even about Flávio himself. It’s also very much about the management of the other Bolsonaro government. He wanted to stage a coup,” he said. “These issues scare me a little.”

Diogo Furtado, a 35-year-old Uber driver in Rio de Janeiro, didn’t vote the first time because of his work, but said he’ll vote blank on Sunday. He said that neither candidate convinced him.

“Brazil needs a new candidate,” Furtado said. “But unfortunately our country has become dominated by two names: Lula and Bolsonaro.”

Lula criticized the U.S. president before Brazil’s election, speaking about the risks of U.S. intervention in Brazilian elections after the Trump administration imposed high tariffs against Brazilian exports and classified drug-trafficking groups as foreign terrorist organizations. Experts say the moves may have been intended to make Lula look soft on crime.

Flávio Bolsonaro has touted his proximity to the White House and insisted that Brazil needs to strike better deals with the Trump administration, including in the rare minerals economy. Brazil estimates that its deposits hold up to 21 million tons, second only to China. He denies he’s being helped by the U.S. government.

Trump said Sunday that he is watching the election but declined to endorse a candidate.

Voting in Brazil is conducted electronically and the result is usually available within hours after polling stations close at 5 p.m. (2000 GMT) in Brasilia.

#104. Drug Enforcement Administration seeks input from Alaskans at Elder's and Youth

The federal Drug Enforcement Administration invited Alaskans at this week's Elders and Youth Conference in Anchorage to share ideas for preventing drug trafficking and overdose deaths in the state.

Marcus Pickett, a community outreach specialist for the DEA, helped run the workshop on Monday and shared information about the risk of addiction and death from fentanyl.

"Drug traffickers and drug dealers don't care what happens to you and your families," he said after sharing stories about how overdose deaths have affected him and his family. "They just want to make money."

Almost 50 people attended the presentation and discussion, where the DEA and U.S. Attorney for Alaska Michael Heyman fielded questions and comments from Elders and youth attending the conference.

That included a comment from Rose Okpealuk, a school bus driver living in Anchorage, who said she has suspected kids of using fentanyl on her bus before.

"I would hear the name 'fentanyl,' and I know that people have been making that a pill," she said. "I've been so scared that I don't want that to be offered to my grandkids that I want to be involved in helping."

Darren Muller Jr., from the village Ouzinkie in Kodiak Island Borough, also shared his thoughts. He said he wanted to understand how people can keep drugs from coming into rural Alaska communities.

"I travel by the Marine Highway System," he said. "And I know that it just seems to me that the state, our state, could do a lot more at that point, where we could maybe try to catch more people with the task force."

He was encouraged to hear in the presentation that the state recently trained about 80 village public safety officers on up-to-date information on how to intercept drug trafficking.

#105. Alaska voters’ privacy could be at risk from state’s handling of voter rolls, judge says

October 21, 2026 by James Brooks, Alaska Beacon

A Superior Court judge said last week that the state Division of Elections may have violated the Alaska Constitution’s right to privacy when it handed an uncensored version of the state’s voter list to the U.S. Department of Justice last year.

On Friday, Judge Adolf Zeman turned down the division’s request to dismiss a lawsuit filed this spring by the League of Women Voters, Alaska Black Caucus and the Alaska chapter of the American Civil Liberties Union.

The three groups are arguing that the Division of Elections and Lt. Gov. Nancy Dahlstrom violated the state constitution when they provided an unredacted state voter list — including driver’s license numbers, home addresses and partial Social Security Numbers — at the request of the U.S. Department of Justice.

As part of the handover, the division’s director, Carol Beecher, signed a memo stating that the division will remove voters from the list within 45 days if the DOJ determined they were ineligible to vote. The memo also says the DOJ may share the data with a private contractor and store it out of DOJ control.

Writing last week, Zeman said that the Alaska Division of Elections failed to negotiate restrictions on the DOJ’s influence over Alaska’s voter list.

“Instead, the state has given DOJ carte blanche to submit whenever it pleases, a list of whosoever names it decides, for whatever reason DOJ determines, for any number of eligible voters,” he wrote.

He said that while the state may point to a clause in the memo that says any removal request must be legal, “the court remains unconvinced that such a clause on its own can shield this matter from judicial consideration.”

The plaintiffs had argued that the Department of Justice is creating an unauthorized national voter database and intends to conduct criminal and immigration investigations not otherwise permitted by law.

The state disputed that claim, and attorneys representing the state argued in September that the three plaintiffs lacked the standing to bring a case, that the case wasn’t ripe because the DOJ hasn’t yet requested the removal of any Alaska voters and that sharing the information would not cause “embarrassment, anxiety, humiliation, harassment, or economic and physical reprisals” among Alaskans.

Zeman wrote that the state may claim that plaintiffs are wrong about the potential abuse of Alaskans’ data, “however, on a motion to dismiss, this court is obligated to accept plaintiffs’ claims as true. Therefore, the court finds that the privacy claims survive dismissal.”

He went on to conclude that “given the fundamental nature of the rights at stake, the seriousness of the allegations and the existence of similar litigation in other states, this court shall allow this case to proceed to discovery and fact-finding that will determine whether plaintiffs’ claims have merit.”

#107. Hill campaign gains ground, but supporters worry it’s too late

More than 100 supporters of U.S. House challenger Bill Hill turned up at a strip mall in Anchorage in early October for a meet-and-greet/crafting event.

The theme was make-your-own Bill Hill campaign swag. Some supporters stamped and decorated t-shirts, signs and caps. Others, like Kathryn Carovano of Anchorage, said they were just there to meet the independent candidate.

She said she gave to Hill early, in part because Ship Creek Group is managing his campaign and she likes other clients the firm has taken on. But, she said, she doesn’t see the same energy that she sees in the campaign of Jonathan Kreiss-Tompkins — a Democrat running for governor, also managed by Ship Creek.

“I don't feel like this campaign has been nearly as ambitious or out there, or you know, it just doesn't have the same momentum. And I don’t know why,” she said.

Hill is an outlier this year. Most polls of Alaska’s other statewide races show Democrats ahead of Republicans, who are tied in varying degrees to an increasingly unpopular president. But Hill, an independent endorsed by the Democratic Party, trails Republican Rep. Nick Begich. His supporters and at least one veteran consultant say avoiding the party brand may have cost him, and the campaign started building momentum late.

Hill mingled with the crowd, then took the mic and told his story. He said he grew up in a tiny village on Iliamna Lake, hauling wood and water and caring for a dog team. As a high school sophomore, he moved to Naknek, in the Bristol Bay region.

“I went from having one classmate in Kokhanock to having 12 in Naknek. It was the big city,” he said, to laughter.

He outlined his career in that school system and his initiative, as a school administrator, to meet the communities’ demand for more shop classes.

Carovano liked what she heard. But she said she expected the campaign to get more aggressive months ago. As early as March, she said, Kreiss-Tompkins was meeting with supporters and asking them to post about him on social media and to tell 15 of their friends about him. She said she tried to do the same for Hill.

“I brought probably 10 people here tonight,” she said. “But it's late for that.”

The Hill campaign is showing signs of increased activity. The candidate has raised about half as much money as Begich this year. But Hill took the fundraising lead in the latest quarterly financial reports. And two recent polls suggest he may be shrinking the gap with Begich. Hill supporters worry it’s too little too late.

The campaign has leaned into Hill’s identity as a fisherman so much that several attendees at his meet-and-greet said they hadn’t heard about his career as superintendent of the Bristol Bay School District. That lack of self-definition gave Begich an opportunity to portray his career as a failure.

“When he came into his tenure as a superintendent, his schools were at 74% math proficiency and 86% reading proficiency. After 10 years, those numbers dropped to 9% and 11% respectively,” Begich said during “Debate for the State” last week. “That's a poor record.”

Hill accused Begich of “cherry-picking” data and offered an indirect defense. He said Congress doesn’t have members who care about schools.

“They won't look at a school that went through a pandemic without good internet in their community, multiple children from foster homes in classrooms,” Hill said. “They will look at a school just through a single number, and that's wrong.”

After the debate, the campaign said the drop in test scores mirrored a statewide plunge after the state changed to a different testing method. The campaign also pointed out that he was named the 2023 “superintendent of the year.”

In a different year, the “independent” label might’ve been a real asset, Anchorage political consultant Jim Lottsfeldt said, but it hasn’t worked out that way.

“In this election cycle, where I think everything is a referendum on Trump, I think this was an excellent year to be a Democrat,” he said.

The Hill campaign is in the unusual position now of asserting that he’s not and has never been a Democrat while a political action committee spending millions to get him elected, Wheel Dog PAC, is running ads associating him with Democrats.

Meanwhile, both the campaign and Wheel Dog PAC are running negative ads on the only Democrat on the ballot: Eric Hafner, an inmate in a federal prison in New York.

Lottsfeldt said the campaign is late to deal with the obvious danger that Hafner’s party affiliation will draw votes away from Hill.

“There should have been a plan to contain or disqualify Hafner in the mind of the voter, and now they're sort of like, ‘Oh yeah, he's on the ballot too,’” Lottsfeldt said. “And now they're sort of scrambling to let everyone know that Hafner actually sits in a prison and has never set a single foot in Alaska ever.”

Lottsfeldt isn’t working on the U.S. House race, but he was a supporter of Democrat Matt Schultz, who reluctantly dropped out in July to clear a path for Hill. That episode was a tough one for Schultz fans like Laura Norton Cruz to get over. The West Anchorage social worker said she was mad at Ship Creek Group for running Schultz off the campaign trail but she likes Hill. She just wonders if there’s time for voter enthusiasm to build.

“I think probably the later start and having a more divided field early on meant that he couldn't coalesce as much support behind him and meet as many people,” she said. “So he's going in an uphill battle.”

At the end of the meet-and-greet in Anchorage, Hill’s campaign manager made a special plea to attendees to take yard signs and swag.

“Please post on your social media that you were here tonight,” Campaign Manager Kevin Groh said. “Take a selfie with your new signs, your stuff. Let people know you were here.”

And, he said, tell your friends to vote for Hill, not for the inmate who appears on the ballot as a Democrat.

#108. Opinion: Alaska needs a fighter, and that fighter is Mary Peltola

There’s been no shortage of ads and mudslinging this election cycle. But strip all that away, and most Alaskans are asking the same few questions. Will my children have a better life than I did? Can I afford to build a good life for my family? Can I cover rent, groceries and the heating bill? And when it comes to these questions, the only candidate for Senate who will actually fight for Alaskans is Mary Peltola.

These days, it feels harder and harder every year for working people to get by while the ultrawealthy and the corporations they run get richer. Alaskans don’t need a study to tell them so. They feel it buying groceries each week and filling up their car. It’s too expensive to buy a home, to rent, to put kids in childcare or even to fill the tank so you can drive them to school. And from Washington, D.C., we’re seeing one injury after another: Our leaders are slashing healthcare and pushing a war that is skyrocketing gas and grocery prices. They are stripping collective bargaining rights from workers and selling out our privacy, our communities and our good-paying jobs to Big Tech CEOs.

That’s why it’s absolutely critical that Alaskans send a fighter to the Senate this year who is from Alaska, for Alaska, through and through. That person is Mary Peltola. She’s on our side.

Mary has a plan to bring costs down. For parents in Anchorage worried about groceries, gas and childcare, she’ll fight to make life more affordable. For Alaskans in the Bush paying over $20 a gallon for gas, she’ll fight to stop the price-hiking war in Iran, refine more oil here in Alaska and restore energy tax credits that create good-paying union jobs and lower prices. For the 10,000 Alaskans who lost Medicaid after Dan Sullivan and D.C. politicians gutted the program, she’ll fight to reverse those cuts.

While D.C. politicians are increasingly bought and sold by special-interest backers, campaign donors and corporate greed, Mary will fight against corruption to make sure our system is actually working for working people.

Mary knows what it’s like to raise kids in Alaska, to fish for subsistence and to worry about paying the bills just like the rest of us.

I’ve had the privilege of knowing Mary Peltola for decades. I can tell you that she is a fighter, and if we send her to the Senate, she’ll fight for Alaska. I know that Mary is driven first and foremost by one key question: How do we make Alaska a better place for all of us, our children and our grandchildren?

Her opponent’s priorities look very different. Dan Sullivan has taken hundreds of thousands of dollars from Lower 48 factory trawlers and special interests. He has tripled his personal wealth in the Senate. Those are not the habits of a senator worried about how Alaskans are getting by. While D.C. politicians side with special interests and corporate greed, Mary will fight corruption and stand on the side of Alaskans.

That is why I will be voting for Mary Peltola for U.S. Senate, as a mom, as an Alaskan, as a veteran and as the former president of the Alaska AFL-CIO. When it comes down to it, I know whose side she’s on. She’s on ours.

#117. Alaska voters’ privacy could be at risk from state’s handling of voter rolls, judge says

A Superior Court judge said last week that the state Division of Elections may have violated the Alaska Constitution’s right to privacy when it handed an uncensored version of the state’s voter list to the U.S. Department of Justice last year.

On Friday, Judge Adolf Zeman turned down the division’s request to dismiss a lawsuit filed this spring by the League of Women Voters, Alaska Black Caucus and the Alaska chapter of the American Civil Liberties Union.

The three groups are arguing that the Division of Elections and Lt. Gov. Nancy Dahlstrom violated the state constitution when they provided an unredacted state voter list — including driver’s license numbers, home addresses and partial Social Security Numbers — at the request of the U.S. Department of Justice.

As part of the handover, the division’s director, Carol Beecher, signed a memo stating that the division will remove voters from the list within 45 days if the DOJ determined they were ineligible to vote. The memo also says the DOJ may share the data with a private contractor and store it out of DOJ control.

Writing last week, Zeman said that the Alaska Division of Elections failed to negotiate restrictions on the DOJ’s influence over Alaska’s voter list.

“Instead, the state has given DOJ carte blanche to submit whenever it pleases, a list of whosoever names it decides, for whatever reason DOJ determines, for any number of eligible voters,” he wrote.

He said that while the state may point to a clause in the memo that says any removal request must be legal, “the court remains unconvinced that such a clause on its own can shield this matter from judicial consideration.”

The plaintiffs had argued that the Department of Justice is creating an unauthorized national voter database and intends to conduct criminal and immigration investigations not otherwise permitted by law.

The state disputed that claim, and attorneys representing the state argued in September that the three plaintiffs lacked the standing to bring a case, that the case wasn’t ripe because the DOJ hasn’t yet requested the removal of any Alaska voters and that sharing the information would not cause “embarrassment, anxiety, humiliation, harassment, or economic and physical reprisals” among Alaskans.

Zeman wrote that the state may claim that plaintiffs are wrong about the potential abuse of Alaskans’ data, “however, on a motion to dismiss, this court is obligated to accept plaintiffs’ claims as true. Therefore, the court finds that the privacy claims survive dismissal.”

He went on to conclude that “given the fundamental nature of the rights at stake, the seriousness of the allegations and the existence of similar litigation in other states, this court shall allow this case to proceed to discovery and fact-finding that will determine whether plaintiffs’ claims have merit.”

In their first public reaction to the proposal, school board members had an emotional — and at times heated — discussion about the proposed ballot proposition ordinance during their Tuesday meeting.

“My fear and my concern is that if we support this ordinance or change in ordinance, we are creating pockets of haves and have nots,” Bellamy said.

The Assembly rejected similar plans in 1993 and 1995. Handeland’s plan, introduced earlier this month, would see Anchorage voters elect a pair of school board members to districted seats in 2028, 2029 and 2030. Board members would be chosen in the same years as Assembly members representing those districts. The board’s seventh member would be elected to an at-large seat in the same years as the mayor.

During an Assembly work session earlier this month, Handeland said the change would strengthen communication between members and their districts, and noted other Alaska school boards that choose members by geographic districts. Currently, school board members are assigned community councils to represent on a rotating basis.

If approved by the Assembly, Handeland’s proposal would put the question before Anchorage voters on the April ballot.

In an interview Thursday, Handeland said he wants residents to determine how to elect school board members.

“If voters are perfectly happy with how we’re electing our school board, that’s fine, but I think voters should have a choice on that,” Handeland said.

During Tuesday’s meeting, Bellamy spoke through tears about her experience attending segregated schools in the 1950s, and said the proposal would force more diverse areas of town to compete with affluent areas for the same district resources.

Higgins said last year’s round of school closures damaged the board’s relationship with the public. A lawsuit filed by families of Campbell STEM students was dismissed in August.

Lessens noted that under Handeland’s proposal, closure discussions over the next four years would include a mix of members elected to both at-large and districted seats.

“To have certain areas of town with a dedicated representative on the school board for their district would be profoundly unfair,” Lessens said.

#124. How Alaskans across the state get their pumpkins — and the traditions they’ve formed

McGrath said donations are what keep the Petersburg pumpkin patch going.

The local grocery store Hammer and Wikan donates thousands of dollars in supplies every year. This year, it donated 200 pumpkins, straw bales and cornstalks. Alaska Marine Lines covers all the shipping costs.

It’s a lot of work, but it’s all for the kids, McGrath said.

“Like the joy in the kids' faces, and every year when they're like, ‘You're the pumpkin patch lady,’” she said. “You have to do it again, right?”

So where do most of Alaska’s pumpkins even come from?

“The journey of a pumpkin begins in a pumpkin patch far, far away,” joked Marc Jones, vice president of merchandising for Alaska Commercial Company.

The grocery store chain supplies 38 rural communities with pumpkins grown in Washington state. Last year, AC stores shipped and sold roughly 4,000 carving pumpkins. Much like other goods coming to Alaska, the pumpkins traveled on a mix of barges, trucks and bush planes to get to communities.

“The Southeast stores, they might arrive in big totes like you often see around town here in Anchorage, but for the stores in rural Alaska, we'll have to put them in boxes and then ship them, and the bypass mail flies on airplanes,” Jones said.

Greg Dahl is the senior category manager for AC. He said for stores off the road system, it can take a few weeks for pumpkins to travel from their distribution center in Tacoma, Wash.

This year, it’s more expensive to ship pumpkins because of the war in Iran, Dahl said. Pumpkins are 30 to 40% more expensive than last year.

“Many, many times of that cost is the freight cost,” Dahl said. “Especially in the bush stores where we're shipping out to say Nuiqsut, which is our most cost prohibitive market to get to by mailing. Gosh, that's a 250% higher cost than the actual cost of pumpkins.”

Despite the higher costs, he said, communities' demand for pumpkins remains.

“It’s something that our customers, regardless of where they're at, they want to enjoy it,” Dahl said. “You know, it's a cultural event here, right? #128. Unalaska's free wellness vending machine is part of an effort to fill rural health gaps

In rural Alaska, finding basic health and wellness supplies can be a challenge. But in Unalaska, a new 24-hour vending machine is a way for anyone to get those resources for free.

The machine is at the Grand Aleutian Hotel, one of the few places on the island that's open all the time. It serves as a gathering place for fishermen, visitors waiting out storms, and islanders stopping by for local fare.

Kayla Lopez, a wellness coach for the Aleutian Pribilof Islands Association, helped install the machine in May. It's in a small room, the kind where you might normally find a machine stocked with ice or soda.

"We have stuff like naloxone, Narcan," Lopez said. "And then fentanyl and xylazine test kits. This was Plan B, and that is gone."

With grant funding from the Indian Health Service, the Alaska Native Tribal Health Consortium partnered with tribal and community organizations to bring these vending machines to communities across Alaska. Besides Unalaska, they're now in Anchorage, Fairbanks, Seward, Juneau, Ketchikan, Talkeetna, Kenai, Metlakatla, Naknek and Kotzebue.

Jackie Engebretson is the consortium's director of behavioral health. She said that in rural communities, there can be significant barriers to accessing health services.

"Someone might have to travel a long distance to get to a clinic, or work around limited hours," she said. "They might even face the stigma associated with asking for certain supplies or purchasing those certain supplies."

Engebretson said the goal is to make sure people can get the resources they need, when they need them.

The consortium helped stock the machines at first, but Engebretson said the project is community-driven. Local organizations manage the machines from day-to-day, and each community can add its own supplies.

Some machines have offered beading and smudging kits, locally harvested tea, and baby diapers.

Engebretson and Lopez said information about what people take from the machines helps them understand what a community needs. Before selecting an item, users complete a short survey on the machine's touchscreen. The questions ask for basic demographic information, as well as whether the user has administered naloxone to reverse an overdose in the last six months.

Lopez said the data helps her organization demonstrate community needs when it applies for grants to keep the machine stocked.

Lopez said the Unalaska machine has dispensed more than 100 items over the last three months, with naloxone and hygiene kits being the most popular. Engebretson said she's seen a similar pattern statewide.

The Aleutian Pribilof Islands Association's health staff led the effort to bring the machine to Unalaska, with Jessica Mata Rukovishnikoff playing a key role. Now that the project's initial grant has ended, the organization is looking for new ways to keep the machine stocked.

Lopez said the organization will continue to get naloxone through Project Hope, a state-run health initiative. Plan B has also been popular — Lopez said the machine ran out of the emergency contraceptive within a week of being restocked.

Unalaskans Against Sexual Assault and Family Violence donates some of the supplies, including Plan B, which can cost around $40 over-the-counter.

For Lopez, making preventive health supplies freely available to anyone, without visiting a clinic, is especially important on an island community like Unalaska.

"It's free because one of the goals is to remove cost as a barrier to accessing important health and prevention resources," Lopez said. "So this vending machine is just the start of things for prevention."

The world’s largest mining company is back in Alaska.

More than a decade after abandoning a search for coal in the western Arctic, Australia-based BHP quietly staked new mineral claims this summer in a remote part of Southcentral Alaska — with an eye for copper.

The company, which owns huge iron, coal and copper mines around the world, also conducted sampling and aerial surveys in Alaska in recent months, a BHP spokesperson told the Anchorage Press.

The project is still in the earliest stages of exploration, likely many years if not decades away from becoming an active mine. Most claims that mining companies stake never actually get developed; a subsidiary of a different mining giant, Rio Tinto, abandoned claims in 2013 in the same area that BHP is now showing interest, according to state records.

But BHP’s foray into Alaska is still notable given the company’s deep pockets and global reach, and as the latest example of the mining industry’s surging interest in the state.

Unlike the small, cash-strapped companies that do much of the claim-staking and early-stage exploration work in Alaska, BHP has the financial horsepower and expertise to turn a prospect into a mine.

“They are not going to farm it out to anyone if they find what they’re hoping to find,” said Dave Szumigala, a state mineral exploration geologist with decades of industry experience. “They’ve got the resources to take it all the way through development.”

BHP’s interest in Alaska comes amid high commodity prices, increased global demand for metals like copper and an intense push by the Trump administration to spur resource development in the state.

The company’s return to the state also reflects an uptick in new mining claims across Alaska, and it could contribute to a backlog in the Dunleavy administration’s processing of those claims.

The number of claims staked this year statewide is on track to double from last year, according to Steve Buckley, head of the mining section at the Alaska Department of Natural Resources.

Buckley's team, which reviews and approves state mining claims, has reported a backlog of more than 10,000 claims this summer. The department has transferred three employees — more than doubling typical capacity — to help work through the backlog, Buckley said.

Buckley declined to comment on BHP’s claims because they are still pending review. But he said large new claim blocks make up “a good portion” of the state’s backlog, which could take months for officials to clear.

State land managers have not yet reviewed and approved BHP’s claims, meaning they don’t appear on the state’s public mining claims database.

But documents filed with the state recorder’s office show that BHP staked dozens of claims across thousands of acres in the heart of the Alaska Range at the beginning of the summer.

Many of the claims appear to be deep in the mountains near the northern edge of Lake Clark National Park and the upper reaches of the Skwentna River, a tributary of the Susitna River, about 100 miles west of Anchorage and the state’s road system.

Szumigala called that region “very prospective” for massive, low-grade copper concentrations known as porphyry deposits.

He also noted that BHP’s interest in the area could draw more investment from other companies. Small exploration firms often stake claims next to majors, he said.

“One way juniors will get investment in their company is to say, ‘Well, we staked right next to BHP’s property,” Szumigala said.

BHP confirmed that it’s assessing the area’s copper potential.

“Through the summer months we commenced non-disturbing studies as part of a first, early-stage exploration program to better understand the area’s geology and resource potential,” the company said in a statement provided to the Anchorage Press. “As we advance this work, we are meeting with local stakeholders to learn more about the region.”

BHP has not applied for permits to conduct more intensive exploratory work like drilling.

“Exploration is inherently a long-term process,” the company said, noting that “the vast majority of exploration programs do not advance beyond the early stages.”

BHP has met with representatives from the region’s Alaska Native corporation, CIRI, to review maps and discuss cultural training, a CIRI spokesperson said.

Environmental groups, meanwhile, say they’re working to ensure that new mining in the state doesn’t come at the cost of public lands and wildlife.

Sullivan been cast instead for Senator Sullivan and Alaska’s rank-choice process played out accordingly, Senator Sullivan would have won the election,” that the Alaska Division of Elections must not certify the election results “pending an investigation to determine whether votes cast for Daniel J. Alaska’s other U.S. senator, Republican Lisa Murkowski, condemned the move in a statement Friday afternoon.

“Alaskans don’t need any help voting for their preferred candidates, and the State of Alaska has already handled this by clearly distinguishing the candidates on this year’s ballot. The Department of Justice should never have filed this lawsuit,” Murkowski said.

Whether the Petersburg Sullivan should be allowed to appear on the ballot has already been affirmed in state courts. The Alaska Supreme Court ruled that Dan J. Sullivan is eligible to appear on the ballot, rejecting an appeal by the director of the state Division of Elections, who previously declared that the namesake challenger filed for candidacy “with a purpose to confuse or mislead.”

The Petersburg Sullivan has maintained that he is running for the seat because he believes the incumbent has not done a good job of serving Alaskans or representing their interests.

The Division of Elections determined how the candidates would appear on the ballot. The incumbent is listed as “Sullivan, Dan S.” with the label “(Registered Republican) Incumbent.“ His namesake challenger is listed as “Sullivan, Daniel J. Jr.,” with no labeled party affiliation.

The case is before Judge Sharon Gleason in U.S. District Court in Alaska.

The lawsuit names Alaska Division of Elections Director Carol Beecher, in her official capacity, as a defendant as well as the state election and ballot-counting review boards.

The lawsuit sets up the state of Alaska, which previously argued that the Petersburg Sullivan’s candidacy was not in good faith, to choose whether to defend the federal government’s calls not to certify election results if Sen. Dan S. Sullivan does not prevail.

State Department of Law spokesman Sam Curtis said in a statement Friday that “the Division of Elections and the Department of Law are focused on running the ongoing election and giving voter’s confidence that their votes will count. This also includes assisting voters and avoiding voter confusion — whether through the information in the Official Election Pamphlet, asking questions of a poll worker, or bringing others to assist you with voting as permitted by law.

The Petersburg Sullivan said in a text message Friday that he had “no comment for now.”

Jonathan Diaz, director of voting advocacy and partnerships at Campaign Legal Center, a nonpartisan organization based in Washington, D.C., said the filing was among “the most frivolous lawsuits” he had ever seen.

“The Department of Justice — which is supposed to represent all Americans, all of us — filing a lawsuit less than two weeks before Election Day, trying to block election results that haven’t even happened yet is so crazy, and is reflective of this administration’s overall approach to the midterm elections, and consistent with a pattern of behavior that that they’ve undertaken over the last year and a half, that shows a total disregard for the Constitution, the law, American democracy and voters,” Diaz said.

The lawsuit’s call for an investigation into voters’ intent is particularly unusual, Diaz said.

“The expression of a voter’s intent is the ballot itself. You cannot then go back and look at a ballot and decide if the voter intended something else. That’s just not how voting works,” Diaz said.

He told reporters on Friday that the lawsuit was filed at his directive.

“I’m the one that said do it,” Trump said.

A spokesperson for Sullivan, Nate Adams, said that the campaign played no role in the lawsuit, and that Peltola’s assertions that Sullivan was involved were “categorically false.”

“We’ll let the DOJ complaint run its course. Alaskans can tell the difference between a Democrat sideshow and Senator Sullivan’s strong record of delivering for Alaska, a record he’ll keep building on for the next six years,” Adams said.

“I feel very much that it’s interference and very corrupt,” Peltola told reporters at the Alaska Federation of Natives annual convention.

“This is the exact kind of corruption Alaskans are tired of, and you know, I think this is a perfect example, 11 days from the election of corruption and desperation, honestly, and a complete lack of respect for Alaskans, and their vote, and the desire for Alaskans to talk about affordability, and how crushed we are getting by these prices on account of the 14 times that Dan Sullivan has voted in favor of this war in Iran.”

Eric Croft, chair of the Alaska Democratic Party, echoed the response from the Peltola campaign.

“Dan Sullivan is scared he’ll lose and that’s why he’s looking to any corrupt, desperate method to win. But let’s be clear: Alaska voters decide Alaska elections. Not politicians, and not anyone from the Lower 48. Every vote will be counted, and Alaskans will make their voices heard at the ballot box in a free and fair election,” Croft said.

“Ranked choice voting has already made Alaska’s elections unnecessarily complicated,” Warfield said. “A candidacy that trades on voter confusion only makes the problem worse and highlights the failures of this voting scheme.”

The Division of Elections plans to release initial tabulated results on Nov. 18. In the weeks between Election Day and the first set of tabulated results, officials will drop preliminary results showing first-place votes, before the ranked choice voting tabulation is completed.

“The entire world is watching, and it’s just two weeks for the president to spiral more out of control,” said Gray.

Gray said he believes the lawsuit falls outside federal jurisdiction.

“Our elections are state controlled; they are not federally controlled, and really, President Donald Trump’s opinions of what should or shouldn’t happen in our elections should not matter,” Gray said. “And I hope that the folks in charge understand that and stand up to him because that’s what it’s going to require.”

Asked in hindsight whether Dan J. Sullivan should have entered the race, given the legal challenges his candidacy has prompted, Gray said he supports the challenger’s right to run.

“The controversy and chaos has been manufactured. It is not something that was caused by Dan J. Sullivan. He had a right to run. He had a right to file. He has a right to be on the ballot,” Gray said.

The history: Van’s son worked for a liquor distributor. Kings X was one of their accounts, and it went up for sale. There were only about 40 people in attendance, so the sharply dressed gentlemen in sports jackets stood out among the rest of the crowd in Alaska summer casual. As suspected, they were Oakland’s John Brothers Piano Company.

The band ripped a few smoking numbers, then invited Bean up to trade licks for a few tunes. Suddenly, we weren’t in Anchorage anymore. The club had been transported to St. Louis, Chicago, New Orleans. Boom. Magical music transcendence.

Personally, I’d like to thank AFD, Enstar and all the folks who worked and volunteered to get this fine establishment back open — and those who are carrying it into its next life. Dive bar is in the eye of the beholder.

Ernie Piper celebrated his 21st birthday in 2008 with a ride on the Gaslight's mechanical bull. The story pitch came in as a text message: What became of the mechanical bull at the Gaslight, the longtime Anchorage nightclub and bar that last month remodeled and reopened under a new name, Club Seven 21.

For the uninitiated: The bull was a staple at the Gaslight Lounge, which itself was a staple, albeit a seedy one, of downtown nightlife for decades. Suffice to say the Gaslight was not a place for the faint of heart — one concise TripAdvisor review notes “strong drinks” and a “rowdy crowd” — and a high-profile fatal shooting outside the bar last year prompted a military commander to ban personnel from the premises.

Nonetheless, the place had its charms, not least of which was its famous, headless mechanical bovine perched on a kind of hydraulic pedestal in the middle of the club. I only visited a few times, but I have an indelible memory of the unambiguously sexual sway of one rider’s hips as she tried to stay on — which I suspect was the point.

An Anchorage Daily News story on the rebrand offered only a hint as to the fate of the contraption: “The mechanical bull has also been removed, leaving more room for dancing.”

So I called John Pattee, the club’s longtime operator, and told him inquiring minds wanted to know what had happened to it.

“The bull was part of the Gaslight, and that’s not who we are,” he said. “It’s a new club, and it’s a new atmosphere, and poor old Oscar didn’t fit in.”

Disappointingly quickly, Pattee solved the mystery: “I still have it. It’s in the back. Maybe you get a thousand readers to say, ‘Hey, we want the bull back,’ I could bring it back if I wanted to. But they need to come in person, buy a drink or two, fill out a form — that the Press provides.”

I could tell Pattee wanted to end the conversation there.

So I said that some people (me) were wondering if the bull was for sale, and maybe the Press could put it in our new office.

“It’s not a toy! It’s a real mechanical bull — it’s got a double horsepower engine. It’s supposed to be a five-horsepower; I added it up to 10,” Pattee said. “It’s out to pasture. But if someone wants to make me an offer, I would look at it.”

Other things I learned from Pattee: He bought his bull in 2008 from a “serious cowboy,” whom he called up after reading about the devices in a news story. Oscar’s creamy white and brown hide is real leather. It was always free to ride, with controls operated by one of his staff.

The settings could go up to 10. “Most people can’t take a two,” Pattee said, but “some of these cowboys, you couldn’t buck ‘em…they could have gone to a 15.”

“And then, of course, there’s all the debauchery over the years that’s happened on the thing,” Pattee added. “Nothing X-rated, but probably — certainly — R stuff on there.”

Liability insurance for Oscar cost $10,000 a year, Pattee said. (He chuckled only a little bit when I said he could have called that part of his policy a “rider.”) There was only ever one lawsuit, according to Pattee — the result of a 2017 spinal injury to an Alaska Airlines flight attendant in training whose depressing prognosis I ultimately absorbed from a thick case file in the Anchorage courthouse next door.

The dispute was settled out of court, though not before citations of mechanicalbull case law and the pre-ride waiver the woman signed — which said the risk of physical and emotional injury “simply cannot be eliminated without jeopardizing the essential qualities to the activity.”

With that, I left the courthouse. Later, I called Cal Perkins — the “serious cowboy” who made Oscar two decades ago, and whose number was miraculously still in Pattee’s phone.

Perkins, who lives in Idaho and is a former professional bull rider, has been making the devices for 35 years, but his enthusiasm for the gig does not seem to have diminished over the decades. More details I learned from him: He sells his top-end mechanical bull for some $33,500. They’ve gone to Italy, Australia and New Zealand, Hawaii. And: “I sent a bull on a barge to Turkmenistan. The foreign oil guys were in there, building up their oil fields or something.”

The Gaslight bull, Perkins continued, had earned some real renown over the years — including with one of his neighbors in Idaho, a journeyman carpenter who’d seen it on a trip to Anchorage. “The city’s well-known, and it’s a great tourism city,” Perkins said. “I would hope that someone would revive it and keep it there.”

Perkins’ neighbor isn’t the only one with fond memories of Oscar. We canvassed Press readers on social media, and several wrote in, including lifelong Anchorage resident Natasha Price.

“In my 20s, I was often witness to the Gaslight’s mechanical bull rides; I didn’t have the fortitude to try it until one night when I had just the right amount of liquid courage,” Price said. “I climbed astride the beast and held on tight. The operator did his best to toss me off, and I probably only lasted less than half a minute. But I dismounted the bull by rolling off it backwards so perfectly that I landed on my feet.

“It was a perfect dismount,” Price added. “Even in my inebriated state, I knew this would be the one and only time I would ever ride a mechanical bull. I could never improve upon that landing!”

Oscar is now in “deep storage,” Pattee said; he sent me a photo of it crammed into a corner, tucked under a pile of mats, to prove it. If you want to bring it back, make him an offer. The Press can’t afford the insurance policy.

#148. Conservatives worry Trump’s Alaska visit may have done more harm than good

It’s been nearly a week since President Trump held a rally in Wasilla campaigning for Republican candidates up and down the ballot. He urged supporters to vote for Republicans in the races for House and Senate and unveiled his pick for governor: business owner Bernadette Wilson.

And since the president’s visit, conservatives have been struggling with one particular question:

“Did he help, or did he hurt?” wondered talk radio host Michael Dukes on his weekday morning show on Oct. 14, a day after Trump spoke in Wasilla.

While conservatives — including Trump — were hoping the president’s visit would provide a boost to Republican candidates, some say they’re not so sure it did.

It would have been an unthinkable idea two years ago. Trump won Alaska by 13 points in 2024, his third time in a row winning the state by double-digit margins.

And for a state with less than a million people, the president talks about it a lot. He has sought to boost oil and gas drilling. He’s offered support for a pipeline connecting North Slope natural gas with customers further south. And Alaska, as conservatives are quick to point out, was the only state with its own executive order issued on Trump’s first day back in office.

At the rally, Republican officials urged the crowd to rank all three conservative candidates for governor: small business owner Bernadette Wilson, former Anchorage Mayor Dave Bronson and ex-Attorney General Treg Taylor.

“There are three of them. I need you to vote for each one of them,” said Gov. Mike Dunleavy, the outgoing Republican term-limited from seeking reelection.

Trump, on the other hand, forcefully rejected Bronson and Taylor. On Truth Social, he said Bronson “begged” for his endorsement and then went back on a promise to drop out. And Trump said he had “never heard of” Taylor, despite his work on Trump’s Alaska executive order.

Instead, Trump put his support exclusively behind Wilson.

“She's the one that won the primary. She's the one that should be able to run singly, one-on-one,” Trump said.

And he didn’t exactly endorse ranking the others.

“You have that ranked choice voting, which is a phony rigged deal,” Trump said. “So, lots of luck, Bernadette. But I'm with you all the way.”

Taylor and Bronson have both declined to drop out, and they’ve disputed some of the president’s claims.

But on conservative talk radio, some hosts are worried the damage might be done. For one, Dukes says Trump’s harsh words for Bronson and Taylor might lead the president’s most dedicated supporters to “bullet vote,” voting only for Wilson.

“There's no way that they're going to rank Bronson and Treg Taylor,” Dukes said. “Because Bronson's a liar, according to the president, and Treg Taylor's a nobody that he's never heard of.”

Joe “Crash” Albrecht, the producer of the Mike Porcaro Show on KENI, called Trump’s comments about Taylor and Bronson “counterproductive.”

“If people are influenced by him, as you say, taking a whack at them, and that deters them from voting for them, that whole thing is hurting the Republican Party as a whole in the state of Alaska,” he said.

The worries extend beyond conservative talk radio. Taylor’s campaign manager, April Gettys, said in an interview Friday that she was worried the president’s detour into the governor’s race might dampen turnout among Republican-leaning voters wary of Wilson. Gettys said she was concerned that could hamper Republicans’ chances of winning the closely watched Senate race.

“Those statements made many voters feel that they just don't even want to vote,” she said. “They feel that those statements just handed the race over to the Democrats.”

Some 57% of Alaska’s registered voters are independents, who list their affiliation as “nonpartisan” or “undeclared.” And pollster Ivan Moore of Alaska Survey Research said voters across the board have soured on Trump. Recent polls show far more Alaskans disapprove of Trump than approve of him, Moore said.

“He's at a minus 21, meaning his disapproval is 21 points higher than his approval,” Moore said ahead of the president’s rally.

To be sure, not all conservatives think the president’s visit hurt Republicans’ chances. In an interview after Trump’s visit, Bronson himself said he didn’t buy the idea.

“I can’t say I agree with that narrow assessment of it,” he said.

And Wilson, appearing on KVNT’s Amy Demboski Show the morning after Trump spoke, said she thought the rally had lit a “firestorm” of enthusiasm.

“There were so many Alaskans feeling like they were confused in this governor's race. ‘My gosh, what do we do?’ They were feeling defeated by it,” Wilson said. “Yesterday gave this shot in the arm, and they realized, all right, now I see where to go.”

Wilson did not respond to interview requests

Trump’s visit at least got Republicans talking, which might translate into better turnout, said Brett Huber, a former Dunleavy adviser who now hosts the political podcast “On Air with Brett Huber.”

But Huber hopes the president’s foray into the governor’s race fades quickly from voters’ memories, he said.

“I hope the impact over time ends up being no impact,” he said.

During the temporary restriction, the trails remain open for hiking and other non-motorized use.

Signs will be posted at the trailheads to notify the public of the temporary restriction. BLM law enforcement rangers and other employees will be in the area to help inform the public about the special restriction. BLM staff will assess trail conditions during May and June and will lift the temporary restriction as soon as the trails have been determined to support off-highway vehicle use.
