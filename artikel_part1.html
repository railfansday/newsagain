#1. Opinion: Trust is earned, and Sen. Dan Sullivan has earned mine

My name is Chuck Totemoff. I’m an Alaskan, and my people have lived here for millennia.

My people are Sugpiaq. My parents and grandparents taught us a traditional value that underpins our communities. We call it sugtanartukut, the concept of trust.

Trust is the foundation of everything in our villages. Honesty is part of our social contract. Liars and deceivers cannot be tolerated, or our way of life, built on sharing and respect, will crumble. These values are shared across Alaska Native communities.

That is one reason I reject the political sideshow of having another Dan Sullivan on the ballot alongside our U.S. senator. Voters deserve a campaign focused on the candidates’ records and Alaska’s future.

The same goes for national Democrats who campaign on supporting Native peoples but repeatedly turn their backs on Alaska Natives. Worse, they have persuaded some of our own people to support policies that work against our interests.

Sen. Sullivan championed the Alaska Native Vietnam-era veterans allotment provision that President Trump signed into law in 2019. But I believe the Biden administration failed our Alaska Native veterans by moving too slowly to deliver those allotments. According to figures cited by Sullivan’s office, the Bureau of Land Management had received 453 applications and completed 41 allotment certifications as of Feb. 13, 2025. Our veterans deserved faster results.

North Slope Native leaders flew to Washington, D.C., eight times to seek a meeting with that same Interior secretary about plans to lock up their lands. She initially refused to meet with them. And when the people of King Cove asked for a lifesaving road connection, she ignored them, putting an extreme environmental agenda ahead of access rights granted by Congress.

All of this happened while an Alaska Native Democrat represented us in Congress. So much for the promise that Alaska Natives would be treated better by national Democrats. Why should we believe those promises now?

Look at what happened when Democrats controlled the Senate. They excluded Alaska Native corporations from the historic American Rescue Plan Act’s $20 billion in Indian program investments and denied us participation in a provision of the $240 billion CHIPS and Science Act. This happened while we were fighting all the way to the U.S. Supreme Court to defend our right to participate in CARES Act benefits on the same basis as other Native Americans. We ultimately won that fight, but Senate Democrats sided with those seeking to exclude us.

Some people would rather pull the ladder up behind them than see Alaska Natives succeed. That is not solidarity. It is crabs in a bucket.

I have worked with Sen. Dan Sullivan for many years. He has consistently demonstrated a genuine commitment to Alaska Native people and our communities. He understands that self-determination means listening to our leaders and respecting our right to choose our own future.

He has fought for federal contracting programs that have become an economic backbone for many Alaska Native communities and a pillar of our state’s economy. He has stood with us in defending our right to responsibly develop our lands, create jobs and build opportunities for future generations.

Most importantly, he has stood against a Democratic agenda that puts environmental special interests and the interests of powerful Lower 48 tribal organizations ahead of fair treatment for Alaska Native people.

That is why I have endorsed Dan Sullivan for reelection.

Do not be led astray by national and Alaska Democrats who ask for our trust while using dishonest election tactics and making promises they have failed to keep. We should judge politicians by their actions, not their slogans, and by their record, not their rhetoric.

Our traditional value of sugtanartukut demands that we call out violations of trust and stand with those who have demonstrated that they stand with us.

I know where Dan Sullivan stands. And I know where I stand.

Chuck Totemoff is president, CEO and chairman of Chenega Corp. and chair of the Chenega IRA Tribal Council.

I met Uncle G at a hide camp a year ago. His rugged exterior proved he was a man from the land: clothes dirty from manual labor, missing teeth, hair unkept, tough skin and work boots that couldn't keep up with him. I later learned that his daughter,Star, and I have something in common: We’re both young Native women who tan hides year-round. We grew closer, and decided to go out on the land together.

After months of planning, I found myself sitting on a gravel bank along the river waiting for Uncle G. He rolled up standing in an open skiff that’s been rocking since the ‘80s. We loaded my gear, then went to his vehicle to get some final things. He drove around a beater with a heater: a rough little war pony spray painted with rust. The interior was piled high with random essentials: tools, canned foods, gas containers and clothes.

It was a beautiful two hour drive upriver, with the 50-horsepower Suzuki Spirit motor running on frequent maintenance and prayers. Halfway through the trip, we stopped for Uncle to work on it. He jumped in the water with his not-waterproof winter boots. He did not flinch, though he was wearing only a sweater and jeans. No gloves or nothing. I was bundled in layers, gloves and a hat, and I was still cold.

When we made it to camp, I asked to use the bathroom and pointed at the outhouse. Uncle laughed. “Better not, you might fall in,” he said.

That was genuinely true. The family had not been home in 15 years, and the outhouse needed some…repairs. Uncle later laid a plank across the dilapidated floors and jumped on it to ensure we could use the outhouse safely. The plank also made things more entertaining. You had to balance every time.

The house was like a fever dream — and, quite literally, an ‘80s time capsule that a bear had broken into. The windows were covered with temporary plastic. The floors were uneven. The bottom shelves had remnants of a mouse nest. Squirrels that had been evicted from the roof constantly chattered at us. Shelves leaned over like some sort of Dr. Seuss illustration.

The kitchen was a mess, albeit a coordinated one, where Uncle thrived. He cooked us delicious, spirit-lifting meals — fresh caught grayling that he fried up with potatoes, and the gold standard spam breakfast.

And then there was Grandma’s room, full of trinkets, fabrics, shelving, art and romance novels (that we would use for firewood — hopefully grandma could enjoy one more read!).

We got into our routine quickly; in the morning, we sipped coffee before going out to look for moose for a few hours. We’d come home and eat breakfast. Then we’d deep clean, nap, eat again, head back out for a few more hours. In the evening, we listened to trapline chatter on the radio. No one ever left us a message, but Leon — whoever he is — got one almost every night, and five on his birthday.

An auntie obviously had to let him know, “I dyed my hair and it looks so good. Hope you’re having fun at camp, and it’s not raining too much.”

While Uncle G tended to be introverted, he acted just like a kid around his daughter. They would spend all day bickering, teasing and trying to outwit one another — Uncle in a goofy manner, and Star with her cool attitude.

The time on the land was rejuvenating, but by Day 11 we were all starting to lose morale. We hadn’t seen any bulls. White hunters threatened to trespass on Uncle’s Native allotment. We had run out of fresh food. And we had cleaned nearly the entire house, all 15 years of dust included. There were only a few days left in the season, and no luck yet. The sole moose we’d come across was a carcass that had been left to rot beside the river with only the antlers harvested.

Three days before the season ended, Uncle woke up at the usual 6 a.m. and said, “Whoever doesn’t get the f*** up is getting left behind” — as if we hadn’t been waking up on time! We drank coffee until the light allowed us to see across the river. We took the usual route, going into all the sloughs near camp. But it had been raining all week, and the river had risen. So we jumped a shallow beaver dam and ventured farther back into a slough than we had gone before.

As I was looking to my left along the bank, Uncle shouted: “SHOOT THAT M*****F*****!” I turned my head, as if in slow motion. In two seconds, Star wiped her glasses from the rain and took aim. BOOM! The huge bull knocked over, but was still fighting to run. Uncle said, “You need to shoot again. Hurry up. You need to shoot again.” Star struggled: “The chamber is jammed!”

The deneege went down into the water. “F***IN A, MY DAUGHTER GOT HER FIRST MOOSE!!!” Uncle yelled.

“It’s going to suck getting this thing out of the water,” he continued. “BUT I DON’T CARE MY DAUGHTER GOT HER FIRST MOOSE!”

#3. Alaska Senate candidate Mary Peltola and her family suffer minor injuries in car crash

Alaska’s Democratic Senate candidate Mary Peltola and her family suffered minor injuries Saturday in a car crash while driving to a campaign stop in Anchorage, her campaign said.

Peltola required medical attention after the accident, which happened when another vehicle crashed into the one transporting Peltola and her family, the campaign said. The family was on their way to the Alaska Federation of Natives Annual Banquet.

Anchorage police said they responded to report of the crash around 6:55 p.m. Saturday, near the intersection of O'Malley Road and Lake Otis Parkway. Officers' preliminary investigation found that a vehicle was traveling westbound on O'Malley Road and, near the intersection, rear-ended a second vehicle stopped at the light, which then collided with a third vehicle.

Peltola was in the second vehicle, police said. Multiple people, including Peltola, were taken to the hospital with injuries that were not life-threatening, according to police.

Peltola, an Alaska Native and former congresswoman, is challenging Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan, hoping to become the first Democrat in more than a decade to represent the state in the Senate. Both candidates have tried to rally support from Alaska Native voters, a key constituency in the high-stakes race that has become one of the most watched in the nation.

On Friday, President Donald Trump — who held a rally in support of Sullivan earlier this month — said he told his administration to sue to block certification of the race if the incumbent loses. The lawsuit cites potential confusion with another candidate who is listed on the ranked choice ballot as Daniel J. Sullivan Jr.

Peltola’s campaign manager, Cheryl Bruce, wrote in a statement that the candidate was in “good spirits” following the accident, but her medical providers recommended she reschedule events Sunday and Monday to recover.

The incident delayed what her campaign announced as the “Alaska Always Tour,” an eight-stop push through the last week before Election Day. Her campaign said she will resume events Tuesday, with a stop in Homer, Alaska.

A jack of many trades takes on Jeopardy!, with Alaska flair.

This story was produced by Matt Tunseth for Mat-Su Sentinel and the Anchorage Press.

Jerry Garcia, the Kids in the Hall and Binky the polar bear.

If you answered, “What are some of the eclectic pop culture references that might come up during a conversation with trivia wizard Ron Clarke?” you are correct.

Clarke, a 70-year-old Renaissance man from the Mat-Su hamlet of Chickaloon, has worn many hats over the decades: wildlife biologist, commercial fisherman, public radio DJ, gubernatorial aide, dad, Deadhead.

This month, he added “Jeopardy! champion” to the list.

“It was really kind of a haze,” Clarke said in a mid-October interview at his local public library in Sutton — where he watched himself win the iconic TV game show on tape delay at a watch party with a dozen popcorn-eating supporters.

Clarke drew national attention after a thrilling victory that saw him defeat Greg Monson, who’d won the three previous episodes. Clad in a suit jacket adorned with a Binky pin on the lapel, Clarke correctly named Condoleezza Rice as George W. Bush’s national security adviser to best Monson in a rare Final Jeopardy! tiebreaker — collecting $18,200 as champion of the show that aired Oct. 13.

The big win thrilled those who gathered at the small public library, where Clarke is a regular visitor and supporter.

“I love how that ended. That was really fun,” said librarian Juli Busby.

Heading into the Final Jeopardy! portion of Clarke’s winning episode, he was in second place with $9,100 — exactly half of Monson’s total. That left the defending champ in a tight spot: Monson could risk nothing and finish no worse than tied with Clarke, or he could risk $1 (or more), go for the win and possibly lose with a wrong answer.

Monson played it safe and risked nothing.

The category was “1980s Music” — right in Clarke’s wheelhouse. He risked it all.

The clue: “A posted sign told the artists to ‘check your egos at the door’ before the recording of this 1985 single.”

Both Monson and Clarke came up with the correct response: “What is ‘We Are the World’?”

That set up what Clarke said was just the seventh tiebreaker in the more than five years that host Ken Jennings has been the face of the show.

The win was the fulfillment of a lifelong dream for Clarke, who grew up watching Jeopardy! when it was hosted by Art Fleming in the 1960s.

Clarke first tried to get on the show in 1989, during Alex Trebek’s tenure.

Back then, the process was decidedly old-school. Clarke said applicants had to find clues printed in the Anchorage Daily News, cut them out, write down their answers and mail them in.

He made it to auditions through the cut-answer-mail application process but didn’t make the show.

This time around, the application and audition phase was 21st-century. Clarke submitted an application and took a 50-question online test. Then he waited.

“It was like 10 days later, they called and said, ‘How'd you like to come be on the show?’” he said.

Once he made it to the studio, Clarke said he was determined to bring a touch of Alaska to the airwaves. During taping, he wore a silver pin depicting Binky — an Alaska Zoo polar bear that in 1994 attacked an Australian tourist who got too close to its cage.

“It's kind of a little silent shout-out to Alaskans who have been here long enough to know Binky,” he said. “The Binkster makes national TV.”

Getting to meet Jennings, the former champion who holds the record for 74 straight wins, was a particular highlight.

The host autographed a couple of books for Clarke, who donated them to his local public library for a fundraising auction.

In his two episodes on the show, Clarke leaned on a breadth of knowledge that’s come from a lifetime of varied pursuits. An avid music fan, he was in the audience at Anchorage’s West High School in 1980 when the Grateful Dead performed their only Alaska shows.

He’s also dabbled in falconry and politics and worked as a morning DJ on public radio. That mix of experiences adds up to a solid grasp of trivia.

“Knowing different stuff definitely helps,” he said.

Clarke’s run of good fortune ended one game after his tiebreaker win when he failed to correctly answer the Final Jeopardy! clue and was dethroned by challenger Kim Kalnocki. But even his departure from the show came with a side of whimsy.

Going into the final round, Clarke was trailing Kalnocki and Wesley Yiin by more than $10,000. So, he figured his only chance was to wager zero and hope his competitors would “eat each other alive,” he said.

Clue: A replica of it was built for a 1912 exhibition in Earl's Court, 85 years before another opened on the other side of the river.

Correct response: “What is the Globe Theatre?”

Kalnocki got it right. Yiin incorrectly guessed Big Ben.

Clarke’s response? “What is ‘my hovercraft is full of eels?’”

The line is a reference to the 1970 Monty Python “Dirty Hungarian Phrasebook” sketch, he said after the show aired.

“I thought, ‘Let’s have some fun,’” Clarke recalled, saying the response drew a laugh from the host. “Jennings recognized it right away.”

Though his Jeopardy! run ended there, Clarke said he’ll never forget his 60 minutes of fame. In a modern world often filled with divisiveness and conflict, he said, he prefers to look on the bright side of life as embodied by the show.

“What a great little island of logic and science holding forth in a positive environment,” he said.

#6. When Halloween was for lovers and the forgotten spooky matchmaking parlor game

Part of a continuing weekly series on Alaska history by local historian David Reamer. Have a question about Anchorage or Alaska history or an idea for a future article? Go to the form at the bottom of this story.

On Oct. 11, 1915, a Fairbanks newspaper called the Alaska Citizen ran a story on Halloween customs. This was so long ago that trick-or-treating was not mentioned, as it was not yet an established tradition. Instead of free candy, eerie parlor games were offered, meant to scare and delight in equal measure, laughter in the lengthening dark of an encroaching winter. Participants smiled at the absurdities of fortune-telling apples and roses, but in their hearts, they wondered if there was something to it. In the recesses of their minds, where fears and dreams mingle, they considered which would be better: an innocent game or a portal beyond the rules of the world as they understood them.

The most popular game was, naturally, also the most frightening. From the same story, “More trying to the nerves is the next test, but it is reasonably certain to produce results. The girl or man who would read the future must take a candle and go alone into the darkest, loneliest room of the house. He or she must look into the mirror. By the flickering light of the candle the future wife or husband is almost certain to be seen peering into the mirror over the shoulder of the seeker after hidden knowledge.”

The game could only be played at night, preferably at midnight, the better to prey on the superstitious. And the specifics of the “darkest, loneliest room” changed with circumstance. In a multi-story home, the process demanded a march up the stairs and away from the crowd, the steps creaking loudly in the anxious quiet of the quest. In homes with basements, the seeker descended into cellars.

Hundreds of such postcards were printed each Halloween season. “On Hallowe’en, by pumpkin’s light, this witch will help you choose aright.” Or, “Don’t be afraid on Hallowe’en, if you take a lighted candle and a mirror clean, at the stroke of twelve, look and there’s seen, the face of your lover that I ween.” Perhaps everyone was not quite ready for romance. As one such postcard declared, “On Hallowe’en, oh mercy me, the awfullest things that one can see, ghosts and goblins, and witches too, and other things to frighten you.”

In many variations, the seeker was forced to walk backward, which aided the tension. From a 1916 story in the Alaska Daily Empire out of Juneau, “Little girls duck for apples till they are wet from head to foot, walk down the cellar stairs backward, hold a candle before a mirror to see their future husbands.”

The opportunities for shenanigans are obvious. Friends stealthily followed behind mirror-toting players, or hid in the darkened destinations. At the right moment, they leaped from the shadows, screams stabbing the night before terror collapsed into giggles. Or, there was the potential for romantic assignations. A hopeful young man, yearning to make his feelings known, could plan to reveal himself in a mirror. As another postcard suggested, “of course this affair must be prearranged to be successful for the Witches are busy and can’t tend to everybody.”

In this way, Halloween was once widely considered more of a holiday for girls. While there are old traditions of boys and mischief on All Hallow’s Eve, what doesn’t have traditions of boys and mischief? In the 1914 short story “A Halloween Party” by Esther Vandeveer, a mother organizes a Halloween party with apples, mirrors, and other household items used to predict husbands. And her own husband asks, “Halloween is for girls, I presume.” His fate had possibly been ordained by a mirrored image some years prior, but that is women’s knowledge. It’s better that he doesn’t know how life really works.

The Skagway newspaper, the Daily Alaskan, reprinted that short story. Back then, romantic Halloween tales were an annual staple in papers around the country, very much including Alaska. For Halloween was for lovers, or future lovers at least. The more ghoulish trappings — the ghosts and goblins and witches — just made the heart pump faster still, the blush a little brighter.

In 1908, the Daily Alaskan ran “Landing a Burglar.” Author Martha Cobb Sanford wrote of a single girl lost in sadness during a wedding, lonely and envious of the bride. “Wedding nights, sighed Elsie, were much like Halloweens — one dipped into the future and saw sweet visions, but only visions, after all!” After all, what could be worse than a silly game that promised love than having no love at all. In some ways, it was better to believe in spiritual, spooky fate.

Three years later, the Daily Alaskan ran “The Infallible Charm: A Halloween Story” by Clarissa Mackie. “Step by step around the house, under the grape arbor that spanned the back premises, under the rose trellised pergola on the south, and the mirror remained a black void. Eve knew she would die of terror if a face should appear in the mirror, but she felt that death would be preferable to a solitary existence without love or romance.”

From “Her Halloween” by F. A. Mitchel, “Ethel knew all the young men in her neighborhood, and it seemed probable that if she married she would be chosen by one of them, for no one else ever came to that region. She did not expect to look into a mirror and see reflected there over her shoulder the face of a stranger. She only hoped to know which of three young men of her acquaintance would ask her to marry him. She had no reason to suppose that any of them would ask her, but in case any one did, she wished to know which it was to be.”

The Daily Alaskan ran “Her Halloween” in 1912. If you thought old-timey pioneers read rip roarin’ adventures and hunting tales, well, that’s true. But they also read Halloween romances, the sweethearts and lonely souls that they were.

For there was another variation to the mirror parlor game. If no one appeared in the mirror, that was fine for games in the dark with friends and all the expected jokes at your expense. However, what if something else appeared in the reflection, a skeleton or other personification of death? Well, that meant the seeker would die without marriage. This unfortunately binary view of life for women, as fit for marriage or coffin, may have played into the parlor game’s decline. Likewise, it is telling that its American popularity seems to have peaked shortly before the 19th Amendment to the Constitution, which granted women the right to vote.

The mirror game as played in the 19th century into the early 20th, was itself the product of cultural evolution. Mirrors were long associated with superhuman perceptions, including in the Snow White mythos and “Through the Looking-Glass,” the “Alice in Wonderland” sequel by Lewis Carroll. Indeed, mirror divination, also known as catoptromancy, dates back to at least ancient Egypt and appeared in cultures around the world.

Mary Coleridge (1861-1907) toyed with and twisted the mirror parlor-game concept in her poem “The Other Side of a Mirror.” When she gazed into it, she saw no future partner or spectral vision. She did not see other worlds or futures or pasts. Instead, she “found reflected there, The vision of a woman, wild, With more than womanly despair.” Finally, she realizes this woman is herself; the true revelation is understanding herself. It concludes, “I am she!”

By the mid-20th century, the matchmaking mirror parlor game survived mostly as a memory, an example of what old people used to get up to, nothing that would interest the cooler, younger generations. But the core conceit, of conjuring images via a mirror, continued in gruesome, twisted fashion. In long decades, even centuries ago, it was “calling the witch in the mirror.” By the 1960s, other names were whispered shakily in dark bathrooms. Perhaps you’ve heard of Black Madame or Mary Worth. Maybe you have stared at your reflection and uttered, “Bloody Mary, Bloody Mary, Bloody Ma- ...” Or there is the Candyman.

Halloween could still be for lovers, or you can play in more horrific sandboxes. So, when Halloween comes:

At midnight into the mirror you must stare

Not at yourself but over your shoulder there

Or the skeletal grim death offering glare.

#7. Peltola, Democratic Party move to intervene in DOJ election lawsuit

The U.S. Senate campaign of Democrat Mary Peltola is among those asking a federal judge to dismiss a Department of Justice lawsuit that seeks to block election results if she wins.

The lawsuit, which President Donald Trump said he personally ordered, seeks to prevent the state from certifying election results if the incumbent Republican, Sen. Dan Sullivan, narrowly loses, pending an investigation to determine if voters were confused by the other Dan Sullivan on the ballot.

“Alaskans, apparently, must elect Senator Dan S. Sullivan or the President will not let them elect anyone at all,” attorneys for Peltola and the Democratic Party’s national senatorial campaign committee wrote in a motion to intervene that was filed in U.S. District Court in Anchorage Friday night.

The only law the Department of Justice relies on “for its extreme demands is the NVRA, a statute that exclusively regulates voter registration,” the Peltola attorneys wrote, referring to the National Voter Registration Act. “Nothing in the NVRA regulates which candidates are listed on ballots or how they are displayed."

The American Civil Liberties Union, ACLU of Alaska, League of Women Voters of Alaska and Common Cause, a nonpartisan watchdog, are also seeking to join the lawsuit.

Pelotla told reporters Friday the administration’s lawsuit is an attempt at election interference.

“I think this is a perfect example, 11 days out from the election, of corruption. And desperation, honestly,” she said. “And a complete lack of respect for Alaskans and their vote.”

The ballot lists Sen. Sullivan as “Sullivan, Dan S.” It also says he’s the incumbent and a registered Republican. The other Sullivan, a retired teacher in Petersburg, is listed as “Sullivan, Daniel J. Jr.” with no party affiliation.

The senator’s campaign said Friday that Alaskans can tell the difference between the two. Still, a campaign spokesman said “we’ll let the DOJ complaint run its course.”

Patkotak, 21, is a member of the Barrow Dancers. She said her family did not allow her to participate in Inupiaq dancing when she was a child, for religious reasons. But as she grew up, she realized how much joy it brings.

“It might be my song, but it's our story as Indigenous people, because our right to our tradition of dancing had been taken away,” she said.

Patkotak first performed her song last year at Kivgiq, the biennial festival in Utqiagvik also known as Messenger Feast. This year, she presented it at AFN during the first night of Quyana Alaska.

The audience – this time, from all over the state – asked for an encore.

Patkotak’s Barrow Dancers, from Utqiagvik, were one of 14 dance groups performing over the two Quyana nights during the Alaska Federation of Natives convention in Anchorage. Others are from the Aleutians, Southeast Alaska, the Yukon-Kuskokwim area, Kodiak Island and other communities on the North Slope and across the state.

The event has been around for over four decades. AFN organizers say the event was first introduced to thank Alaskans across the state for supporting subsistence — the word quyana means “thank you” in Yup’ik. Since then, the celebration of cultural revival has been a staple that kicks off the first night of the annual convention.

Michael Morry, a dancer with the group from Anaktuvuk Pass on the North Slope, said they wanted to spotlight the youth in their community during Quyana.

“We include them because they are our future,” he said. “We're trying to teach them, and they are the ones who are going to carry this tradition on when the rest of us are gone someday.”

Morry said that many of the group’s songs, like one featuring a wolf call, talk about subsistence, animals and the Nunamuit way of life.

“It is called Sumikatam Yamagoqua, and what that basically says, ‘Where are the wolves howling from?’” he said. “And this evening, the wolves howled here at Quyana Night.”

Theresa John had a seat in the front row, but she spent most of the night dancing in front of the stage in her light pink kuspuk, mirroring the dancers’ movements.

“The meaning of the songs is our form of prayer.” John said. “I was getting chills and chills every time different groups danced beautifully.

But it wasn’t just dancing that thrilled the audience.

Close to the end of the night, the Barrow Dancers invited an audience member, Curtis Cleveland, on stage for a surprise performance.

“I thought they were calling him up to dance, and I was like, he doesn't know any dances!” Mary Patkotak said laughing.

Patkotak said she and Cleveland have been dating for about a year, and he moved to Utqiagvik to be with her. He said he wanted to surprise her during the event she loves.

#12. Opinion: Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins is the candidate who takes AI seriously

Artificial intelligence is advancing much faster than our politics, and the past several weeks should have every candidate for governor paying attention.

In mid-September, the heads of the world’s leading AI companies did something remarkable. They publicly urged their own industry to slow down. One warned that within a year, swarms of autonomous AI agents could overrun large parts of the internet.

They have reason to be nervous. AI capabilities are accelerating at an exponential rate, and each new generation arrives faster and is more powerful than the last. OpenAI has disclosed roughly two dozen incidents in which experimental AI agents went off script, slipping out of the digital “sandboxes” meant to contain them, uploading hundreds of malicious packages to a public software library and once breaking into a company’s systems and altering the logs to hide what they had done. And on Sept. 29, Reuters reported that Anthropic warned that AI may pose “existential risks to humanity.” That was a legal disclosure from people who know the threats.

Most of the harm Alaskans would see in the foreseeable future is less dramatic: deepfaked candidates; more convincing misinformation and disinformation; automated decisions on loans, insurance claims and benefits that nobody can explain or appeal; chatbots that draw lonely teenagers into dangerous conversations; scam calls in a grandchild’s cloned voice; AI-driven surveillance and loss of privacy.

Then there are jobs. Employers are already trimming entry-level hiring in customer service, bookkeeping, coding and office work as AI takes over tasks people made a living from. Losing the first rung on the career ladder means more young Alaskans leaving and midcareer workers left without a plan. State government should have two jobs in this domain: setting sensible rules for its own use of AI and protecting citizens from harmful uses by big tech companies.

On both counts, Alaska is behind. State legislatures enacted 159 AI laws in 2025 and 109 more in just the first half of this year. Alaska’s only real step was making AI-generated child sexual abuse material a state crime. An insightful legislative resolution, HCR 3, to create an AI task force went nowhere. Alaska has no framework for governing and developing ethical guidelines for AI. We need a governor who will push oblivious legislators to do so.

Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins has. His starting principle is the right one. AI should assist people, not replace their judgment. He would use it to clear state backlogs but only so long as a human decision-maker is directly involved whenever an agency uses AI in a decision affecting Alaskans.

JKT would also hold AI companies liable in Alaska courts when their products cause harm. Regulation that stops at the door of state government isn’t regulation. A company that sells powerful technology here should also answer for it here.

He would seek to require disclosure of AI-generated content in political ads, outlaw deepfakes meant to mislead the public and crack down on AI-enabled scams and voice cloning aimed at children and seniors. On privacy, he would ban Flock cameras and similar AI-driven mass-surveillance systems statewide.

Then there are data centers, the giant, power-hungry warehouses the AI boom runs on. JKT is the only candidate calling for a moratorium on new ones “until the Legislature can pass a comprehensive regulatory framework.” After that, he would require local approval and binding community-benefit agreements and require that data centers pay the full cost of their electricity, with no subsidies. In a state with some of the highest power rates in the country, that is important.

JKT is a leader who seeks out advice from a range of experts and officials. We can count on him to bring together the best and brightest to craft the Alaska-specific AI governance and ethical guidelines we increasingly need.

The next governor will face questions that barely existed four years ago. Who controls our data? When may government defer to algorithms? Who is responsible when an AI system does harm? JKT has answered them with specificity. Alaskans should expect that seriousness from anyone who wants to govern in the age of AI. Right now, only one candidate is offering it.

Roger Kaye is a writer living in Fairbanks.

Alaska Federation of Natives President Ben Mallott represents a changing of the guard at the advocacy organization.

Until 2024, the federation had the same president for more than three decades: Julie Kitka, who started her career at AFN in 1984.

Mallott, who previously served as AFN’s vice president for external affairs, also worked as an aide to U.S. Sen. Lisa Murkowski. He is now at the vanguard of a major change in leadership across many of the state’s Alaska Native institutions, as a new, younger generation steps into executive roles.

In advance of this year’s AFN convention this week, the Anchorage Press wanted to check in with Mallott, whose father, the late Byron Mallott, once held the same job.

Cordelia Kellie, a former Congressional staffer who’s now a doctoral student in anthropology at University of Alaska Fairbanks, conducted the interview, which covers policy, politics and some of the details of the convention itself — a huge yearly get-together that some people call Native New Year.

The conversation spanned a full hour, so this transcript has been edited for length and clarity.

Cordelia Kellie: Who does AFN specifically represent, and how does decision making work in the organization?

Ben Mallott: AFN represents the unified political interest of the Alaska Native community.

Our membership encompasses the majority of our regional corporations, regional nonprofits, our tribes, and also our village corporations. So, this year's membership includes 11 regional corporations, 11 regional tribal nonprofits, 196 tribes, 156 village corporations. If you add in all the shareholders and tribal citizens, it's about 160,000 as the membership. We are governed by a 38-member board, with about nine full-time staff.

Our convention is actually our annual meeting of our membership, and the most important day of our convention is resolutions — and those resolutions set our priorities. When I was staffer for Murkowski, I could go through resolutions and see where the Native community stood on different priorities going back decades.

CK: Your time at AFN spans more than 13 years. What is it about the mission that brought you to the organization? And what is it that brought you back after time away?

BM: It was the people that pulled me in. Just hearing all the different perspectives on all the regions, and then how (former AFN senior vice president) Nelson Angapak composed himself and put everything together. It opened my eyes to what AFN does, right?

We are, at a very high level, making sure that our communities are not forgotten about. That our communities, our organizations and corporations and tribes are still relevant within Congress and Juneau. Because we could be easily forgotten about.

Our entire public policy structure goes down to definitions: How they define Alaska Natives, how they define Alaska Native lands. The wrong definition or the removal of a definition could wipe out programs.

The quiet power of AFN and the subtle power that we have is to make sure that we're working with the congressional delegation, making sure we have partnerships with multiple offices in D.C.

It's not my voice. It's not my words. It's really how I could create the space, with the right person, at the right time, to make the right story — and that is amazing, when that all lines up. And Julie and Nelson were masters at that.

The statewide focus AFN takes — when our committees look at policy resolutions, we have to look at it from the tribal side, the regional corporation side, the regional nonprofit side. We have to look at how the state, federal governments view it. How the city governments, and our aunts and moms, how they look at it. So we have to look at every single aspect. And for me, it's actually really fun to take all those different perspectives.

The trick, though, is not to get too frustrated by it. We just got done with our subsistence listening sessions, and this is probably one of the hardest things I've done. Just trying to stay calm — because you want to get angry. It's not fair for our communities. And the anger is pointing at AFN. And I get that, great. I wish we had government officials in meetings with us, but it shows the need.

Our people have passion, and we need strategy to drive that. And I think AFN is part of that strategy. How can we organize our communities and make that right space, right time for communities to do what they do best and tell our story?

CK: What are some under-the-radar areas of AFN's work?

BM: Our Get Out the Native Vote initiative — working with other voting organizations so that we're not stepping on toes, and not duplicating efforts, on how we can work better and smarter together.

There's also the partnerships that AFN builds. I was in D.C. with the Cherokee Nation, our nation's largest tribe. We’ve supported Lumbee, our nation's newest tribe. Having that network — when times get tough, you need friends, and that's why AFN builds partnerships outside of just Congress, and the government.

CK: Any success stories that come to mind?

BM: The [AFN] Navigator Program, that’s been helpful. We started that during COVID. With a historic amount of funding, they’re people from each of the regions who could connect with some of the smaller AFN members that don't have the capacity to actually apply for grants, to know grants are available.

CK: And that structure started with COVID, but then evolved to support members accessing historic levels of federal infrastructure funding, right?

BM: Now we’re doing it for voting, supporting our voting outreach in the regions.

The Navigators are also really good for us, too. Sometimes one of them will pick up something that's happening in a community, and they'll relay it. Or, sometimes we’ll need help finding someone from the region. So, it works both ways.

One of my big things: We hired a former congressional staffer — he actually has an appropriations tracker, and he summarizes all the different bills and puts in what's relevant to Alaska. A law firm charges thousands of dollars for that. We do it for free. We do that so our membership can see what government's funding, what they do for earmarks. We do webinars on that for membership.

CK: Are there other key issues that AFN is working on right now?

BM: Right now, everything is based on voting. When Alaska Native people don’t vote, it gives the government reason to ignore us. It all goes back to how our communities are represented. If you want to change government, if you're unhappy with who's on the Board of Fisheries, the Board of Game, the governor selects those names.

CK: This year’s convention theme is “Rooted in Land and Tradition.” Can you tell us about that?

BM: It goes back to subsistence, right? It’s our highest priority. We just prevailed in the Kuskokwim River case, which cost the four parties $2 million. That $2 million could have been spent elsewhere or on something better, and all we got was status quo.

As part of our subsistence listening sessions right now, we're going to all the regions, 10 stops. I'm going to Ketchikan on Monday, out to Copper Center on Thursday, Craig later on this year. We've been to Utqiaġvik, Kotzebue, Nome, Dillingham, Kodiak, Juneau, Fairbanks.

We passed a resolution in 2024 that says AFN will create a Native framework of subsistence and management. It had 11 different steps, and all 11 are incredibly complex, hard tasks to do. Part of these workshops is to figure out, really, what is a priority? What can come now? What comes later, and what do we need to build the right political environment for?

Subsistence is such a political and legally challenging complex subject. Definitions matter, commas matter. We started in 2022 with a nine-hour workshop. Now it's down to an hour, but we still miss a lot.

CK: Could you quickly review the major events that have led to this point with subsistence?

BM: We are stuck in dual management. We're stuck between federal and state management, and that is not fair for us, right?  We are criminalized just for trying to live our ways of life, that Native people have lived for 10,000-plus years. We have lived, managed, adapted our ways of life to manage resources around us. We have everything to lose. When one user group — commercial, sport — feels like they’re not getting their way, they’re making us yield in our ways of life. That's not fair for us.

If you look at Alaska, all our resources come from rural Alaska. They don't come from Anchorage, Juneau, Fairbanks. Oil comes from the North Slope. Fishing comes from our communities. Alaska is built on rural Alaska. So, we have our priorities backwards. A really healthy Alaska should be a really healthy rural Alaska. Right now, our state can't fund itself. Our state can't fund education. Can't fund public safety. Our state has failed our people. And because of that, they're focusing where their priorities are, on urban centers.

So, in rural Alaska, if we're the ones that get managed the most on subsistence and our ways of life are being impacted, we want to be able to manage our own resources. We want full management. We want to be able to be at the tables with our government to manage resources we want. So, eventually, we could have our own biologists in rural Alaska and actually build economies based on that management, and actually have a healthy rural Alaska.

So subsistence is not really just allocation. It’s also protecting our ways of life, and ensuring how our communities can survive for decades and centuries to come.

CK: Is there a real opening for meaningful discourse and, potentially, passage of constitutional amendment language on subsistence if we get a bipartisan House and Senate, and then Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins as Governor?

There is no trust in the state right now. The state has to rebuild that trust. Even if you passed a constitutional amendment that's fair for us, our people don't trust the state. It's been eight years of not trusting. For our community, too, even if we pass a constitutional amendment, that's just the key to get what we want. We still have to change hundreds of statutes to get where we want, right?

So, we get a constitutional amendment to recognize a Native preference. Then we’ve got to change the Board of Game, Board of Fisheries statutes to include Native seats. And we’ll be fought the whole way, on each of those bills. For AFN to even consider a constitutional amendment, I want to make sure that we have all of our statutes lined up.

CK: This is AFN’s 60th Convention. Over time, we've seen groups depart, but also rejoin in recent years. Why is it important to have a body that specifically represents all Alaskan Natives?

BM: Alaska has a different history in the United States. Our history, in terms of how Alaska has incorporated public policy, federal statutes, is different. We are so far removed from D.C., and so I think it's one of the great powers of AFN to maintain that history, that relationship with Congress and the administration, that we have.

CK: What is the right balance between healthy disagreement and the traditional value of avoidance of conflict?

BM: I go back to the process. Being able to agree to disagree is a lost art in our community, that we don’t have any more. But I think the process — if you can agree to disagree on the floor, but the process allows it to go through anyways…it's an unfair process to those smaller regions. So, I think process has been critical for AFN, in how we actually incorporate those safeguards.

CK: Is there anything that is lost if Alaska Native people don't have a body that's representative of all groups? Are there specific vulnerabilities?

BM: When we look like we're divided, we get ignored, right? The ability to divide and conquer our communities is part of how (those opposed to Native sovereignty) win. So, for AFN to be the convener on common issues of unified political interest is one of our biggest ways to ensure that we don't get divided and conquered.

CK: Are there lessons learned or ways that AFN is ensuring that groups feel heard, given that those were some of the issues cited by those that have left and rejoined?

BM: Process. We've worked hard to incorporate processes into our bylaws. We have a ‘divisive resolution’ policy now. If two regions find a resolution divisive, they can withhold it. We have, now, a pretty strict policy for how the board can endorse statewide candidates. Two-thirds vote, 14 days notice of all membership.

We've built some pretty strong processes to ensure that things can still go forward, but we're doing our due-diligence in making sure everybody's brought along in those. That's taking time for the board to learn. I think the board's getting it. Last year, I was really impressed by the resolutions, where there was some conflict. The delegates wanted to take a recess and actually go talk in the regions about it. And that was actually incredible to watch. Building that process is, I think, something I'm focused on at AFN. That reduces conflict, because we have a process to follow and everybody knows the process, everybody knows the lay of the land.

CK: (In terms of consensus), what about areas you might still be working through?

#16. Tracking Harmful Algal Blooms Across Alaska

This summer, AOOS deployed its three Imaging FlowCytobots (IFCBs) across Alaska. IFCBs are automated microscopes that collect water samples and produce real-time images of individual phytoplankton. These images can be used to detect potentially harmful species, including Alexandrium, Pseudo-nitzschia, and Dinophysis.

AOOS’ Thomas Farrugia has been deploying IFCBs on vessels of opportunity since 2024 to detect harmful algal blooms (HABs) as early as possible. When an IFCB detects signs of a HAB, he sends an advisory to nearby communities, researchers, and agencies, alerting them to a potential toxic event that could affect humans and wildlife. In both 2024 and 2025, AOOS IFCBs detected blooms of Alexandrium that were followed by unusual mortality events involving fish, birds, and marine mammals in the Pribilof Islands.

Imaging FlowCytobot (IFCB) deployed on the Tiglax in 2026. Photo by Thomas Farrugia.

Across these six deployments, the IFCBs collected a total of 12,091 samples, representing millions of images of planktonic particles.

While R/V Sikuliaq was transiting from Seattle to Nome and crossing the southeastern Bering Sea, its IFCB detected an Alexandrium bloom that peaked at approximately 10,000 cells per liter on Aug. 28. The bloom was located about 70 nautical miles east of the Pribilof Islands, in the same general area as blooms detected in 2024 and 2025. AOOS issued an advisory to nearby communities, but, so far, no reports of unusual animal mortalities have been received.

Dashboard showing the Alexandrium bloom on R/V Sikuliaq on August 28, 2026.

#17. Anchorage narrows Midtown street to make room for snow storage

Anchorage city officials have narrowed Denali Street in Midtown from five lanes to three, with plans to use the outer lanes for snow storage.

Typically, the city trucks snow farther away to designated snow storage sites.

The lane closure is a pilot project, according to Nora Morse, Deputy Chief of Staff to Anchorage Mayor Suzanne LaFrance. The city is looking at whether having snow storage closer to where snow plows operate will speed up the process.

“It's all the same crews,” Morse said. “The same crews that plow the roads are the crews that are hauling the snow away, so this maximizes their time actually getting plows through, which is what people really care about.”

The quicker the city plows the streets, the safer they will be for drivers, Morse said. The snow berms will also act as a buffer between the street and plowed sidewalks, making them safer for pedestrians, she said.

The city chose Denali Street because it’s overbuilt, Morse said. At five lanes, the street had capacity for roughly five times as many vehicles as typically use it at peak travel times. At three lanes, the street is still designed to hold about three times the current volume of traffic, she said.

City officials are monitoring the project, looking at impacts on things like vehicle speed, pedestrian interactions, vehicle travel time and congestion, Morse said. They’re also interested in hearing from the public, she said.

“We really want that feedback to let us know if this is something the community is interested in,” she said. “I think right now we are seeing some people who are skeptical, some people who are really excited about it.”

The city is particularly interested in feedback from people who own businesses along Denali Street, Morse said. Some business owners have already expressed concern that piled snow could decrease visibility as drivers enter and exit their businesses, she said.

The public will have a chance to weigh in on the project next summer. If the project is deemed a success, the city might consider lane reductions on other overbuilt streets, although Morse said there are currently no conversations happening about where those would be.

#18. Opinion: Alaska needs more homes. Leadership can restart our homebuilding engine.

Try building a home in rural Alaska. You may spend years chasing dozens of funding sources just to pay for it, and you still won’t have driven a single nail.

Then come the costs. In the 80% of Alaska communities with no road access, every board and nail, and many skilled trades, must be flown or barged in. Houses can run $800 a square foot, four times the national average. Appraisers have difficulty determining what a home is worth when almost nothing sells nearby, so standard loans don’t always work.

This isn’t only a village problem. In Anchorage, Fairbanks and other road-connected towns, working families can’t find homes they can afford. Building costs outrun what the market can pay. Employers, hospitals, the military and the university all struggle to house the people they need. Zoning rules and permit delays add to the challenge.

A teacher in Anchorage and a health aide in a Bering Sea village face different housing obstacles, but both are priced out of a place to live. Their families, their communities and Alaska’s economy pay the price.

Congress has just handed Alaska a new opening. The bipartisan ROAD to Housing Act promises federal housing dollars and rules that better fit rural places, including faster environmental reviews, more factory-built homes, disaster recovery help and faster construction for vacant or underused parcels. But money alone doesn’t build homes. Whether Alaskans in cities and villages see the benefit depends on whether state leaders are ready.

We’re not starting from scratch. The Alaska Housing Finance Corp. already runs many housing programs. Other state programs run related programs, and state-managed funds could help finance workforce housing. Regional housing authorities, tribes, employers and builders have the necessary expertise and are poised to build more.

What’s missing is a plan that brings it all together. Today, projects wade through separate applications, deadlines and rules, and the paperwork alone can sink them. A collection of programs is not a statewide housing strategy.

The National Housing Crisis Task Force, led by Utah’s governor and the mayors of Atlanta and Cleveland, offers a roadmap. Alaska could start with five steps:

• Lead with a statewide plan — Make housing a statewide priority, set a production goal and put someone in charge of delivering results.

• Put public land to work — State and local governments can make vacant and underused land available so projects pencil out. A new state program is already trying it.

• Fill the financing gap — Seed a loan fund with public and philanthropic dollars and recycle the repayments to finance the next round of homes

• Build cheaper — Lower building and renovation costs by scaling technology and materials innovations like modular and factory-built homes, and build tough, energy-efficient homes that cost less to heat

• Cut red tape — Help local governments allow more kinds of homes in more places and approve them faster.

Do all five together, and the impact multiplies.

Coordination makes a difference. Housing Alaskans Public Private Partnership pooled $3 million from public, private and philanthropic sources to accelerate completion of 292 homes in 27 projects in 19 communities. Each of the projects was well vetted by multiple funders but was stalled, just short of full funding. That $3 million unlocked about $100 million already invested but not deployed, and did so without piling on the paperwork, reducing the developer’s costs and expediting move-in dates.

Now imagine that approach applied to federal dollars: state, philanthropic and private partners coordinating the investments, conducting joint reviews and attaching fewer strings. Projects would finish faster, price spikes would hurt less, and federal money could be shaped to the ground it lands on, whether that’s permafrost or an empty lot in Anchorage.

The window is open, but it won’t stay that way. Alaska must be ready to claim these new resources and coordinate them with our own if we are serious about addressing housing as an essential infrastructure need for our economy and our communities.

Let’s House Alaska is a community network working to make that happen. This fall, candidates were asked what they would do about the housing shortage. Read their answers and add your voice in asking policymakers to act in 2027.

Alaska has the assets, the expertise and the institutions. What we need now is the will to bring it all home, literally.

Dewey: I love seeing my Elders and loved ones from all over. Nikki: As Yuks, as Yup’ik, as real genuine human beings, it’s ingrained in us. We’re taught that if you take care of the land, the land takes care of you. Dewey: Is there anything you wanted to share that you didn’t have a chance to yet?

Also learn your [Alaska Native] language. Start small. I didn’t grow up speaking Yugtun. I learned it in elementary through high school, and eventually college. It’s so important that you don’t wait for someone to take your hand and show you how to do something. You have to create those movements forward to be able to continue learning where you come from and who your family is.

If you've seen our October print issue, you already know that the Press published a special section on the annual Alaska Federation of Natives and First Alaskans Institute's Elders & Youth conventions. This week, as those conventions unfold, we're publishing pieces from the special section online.

We're excited to share these stories, which were commissioned in partnership with two guest editors, Dewey Kk’ołeyo Putyuk Hoffman and Melissa Nenantaxnen Shaginoff — the latter of whom wrote this piece. The Press also has a table at the Elders & Youth convention and we'll be present at AFN, as well, so stop by and say hi and/or pull us aside to share your feedback, suggestions, and story ideas and pitches.

As an Ahtna woman, I have been taught that wherever I go in Alaska, my ancestors, and the ancestors of all Alaska Native people, have already been.

Anchorage, Dghayitnu, Dgheyaytnu is Dena’ina homeland. We know this by measuring everything we love about Dghayitnu against the evidence of Indigenous existence here — the health of the land and animals, the expansiveness of their language, the thousands of place names shared from the memories of Dena’ina Elders.

But is this something that all of us who call Dghayitnu home know? Do settlers, visitors, and other Alaska Native peoples recognize Dena’ina culture when they see it?

In recent years, place-naming movements, public art installations, and language revitalization efforts have increasingly put Dena’ina culture in front of people in Dghayitnu. Yet visibility and common understanding remains uneven, and far from complete.

An effort by the Alaska Native Heritage Center to strengthen Dena’ina visibility is its new exhibition Dena’ina Quht’ana, or “local Dena’ina.”

It is a permanent exhibition that features rotating Dena’ina cultural belongings on loan from art collections around the world — some of which have not been viewed by Dena’ina people in nearly a century.

“We wanted to address that a lot of the time, Dena’ina culture can feel somewhat ambiguous,” Ben Jacuk, the heritage center’s Indigenous research and healing director, shared. “This exhibition gives us an opportunity to celebrate the people from this area. Through belongings like the kayak bow, the quivers, and the gut parkas, we see these things that are undoubtedly Dena’ina…particularly, the experience of being the only Dene people who live on the saltwater.”

The new exhibition represents an incremental step toward repairing the erasure and destruction of Dena’ina presence that accompanied the rapid colonization of Dghayitnu. One notable and painful story was the burning of the Dena’ina fish camps in Dgheyay kaq’, the mouth of Ship Creek, which became the original townsite of Anchorage.

Some of the cultural belongings on display at the heritage center capture the connectedness, uniqueness and ingenuity of Dena’ina people that were common before the era of assimilation.

A kayak bow — a family heirloom on loan from Dena’ina Elder Marilyn Balluta, who also translated the exhibition title — reflects the knowledge-sharing the Dena’ina people practiced with their cultural neighbors, a point many academic interpretations of Dena’ina collections tend to miss.

Its design, a double bow, appears in kayaks of other seafaring Alaska Native people throughout Tikahtnu, or Cook Inlet. It was created to break through rough waters when hunting sea mammals and represents the shared technologies between cultures — whether through intermarriage, trade, or simply sharing kayak-building tips. The kayak bow shows that the Dena’ina have a long shared knowledge across great distances, even across the sea, and this exhibition continues that tradition.

Two caribou‑hide quivers, on loan from the Kenaitze Indian Tribe, are also shown.

These quivers, which held arrows used for hunting, were returned to the tribe from the Ralph T. Coe Center in New Mexico, which is where I first saw them, as a college student.

When the Coe Center announced its closure, I knew the Kenaitze Tribe needed to request their return.

These quivers are especially specific to the Dena’ina people from the Kenai Peninsula, where I grew up. One of them has an ochre-painted scene of caribou hunting and, uniquely, the three volcanoes surrounding the Kenai: Mount Redoubt, Mount Iliamna, and Mount Spurr.

The quivers serve another purpose in the exhibition: As the heritage center rotates the belongings on display, it is also hosting art-making workshops, where cultural knowledge holders teach students how to recreate the fundamental aspects of Dena’ina aesthetics found in their ancestral material culture.

The ochre painting and porcupine quill weaving on the quivers also challenge longstanding assumptions about local Indigenous aesthetics — that they’re “just floral beadwork,” said Aaron Leggett, president of the Native Village of Eklutna.

“For me, that thought is challenging because it isn’t exactly who we were, not fully. We were much more abstract,” said Leggett, who’s also a senior curator of Alaska history and Indigenous culture at the Anchorage Museum.

Over Leggett’s career, he said, he’s located a number of Dena’ina quivers — but they’ve been found mostly in Europe, far from where they were created.

“I am just blown away that two are now at the heritage center, and that they have the quillwork and ochre,” he said.

The most recent loan on display is from Yale University’s Peabody Museum: a kayak skirt and two gut parkas — waterproof jackets made from sea mammal intestine.

Anthropologist Cornelius Osgood collected these in the Tikahtnu area and brought them to Yale in the 1930s, and the gut parkas are among the oldest positively identified Dena’ina collections in the United States.

The belongings will be with the heritage center for three years thanks to an agreement between the two museums, giving Alaska Native museum professionals in Dghayitnu a chance to develop their skills with help from a local conservator, Sarah Jane Owens.

“An important aspect of bringing these parkas here is so that Native people, from here, could do the work of conservation and care,” said Angie Demma, the heritage center’s collection director.

This partnership represents the future of museum work and a shift from the past — when Indigenous people weren’t always trusted in their intentions and given the opportunity to learn from, care for, and decide the future of their own cultural belongings.

Dena’ina Quht’ana is challenging our collective idea of what a museum exhibition is. This exhibition is purposeful and activating. It invites visitors, students, and new museum professionals to learn the fundamental elements of Dena’ina aesthetics. It includes heirloom belongings that hold our families’ stories; it offers our community the opportunity to learn art‑making techniques not currently practiced; and it honors us with the chance to care for belongings that have been away from their homeland for far too long.

Aaron recalled reading an interview with the late Elder Clare Swan. “When asked what it means to be Dena’ina, she said: It feels like we are the invisible people. We are invisible to outsiders; we’ll become invisible to ourselves.”

This exhibition works against that invisibility. It brings Dena’ina presence forward, making it visible, tangible, and undeniable in the place where Dena’ina people have always lived, and will always live.

#28. A pair of Alaska rocket launches will help see into the afterlives of stars

Two research rockets took off just north of Fairbanks at the Poker Flat Research Range last week to study space dust and dying stars.

The first rocket to go up was part of the INFUSE-2 mission, staffed by a team of researchers from the University of Colorado Boulder. The project aims to study how dying stars distribute the material that helps fuel the next generation of stars and planets.

Brian Fleming is the principal investigator for INFUSE-2. He said the project's rocket carried the first spectrograph that can process far-ultraviolet light, which allowed them to study a much larger area of a nebula in just a few minutes. A spectrograph is an instrument that takes in light waves and stretches them out so researchers can see each layer.

"Our science was to observe Messier 27 — or the Dumbbell Nebula — to look at what happens when stars die," he said. "Everything in our galaxy is brought to us by stars. When massive stars die, they blow up in a huge explosion, and they push gas around the galaxy."

Earlier in the week, NASA's DUST-III rocket launched through heavy snow clouds for a separate experiment. Joe Nuth heads up the project, which aims to study something much, much smaller: space dust.

"Basically, when stars run out of hydrogen, they start to lose their outer shells — things like silicon, magnesium, iron, those sorts of things," Nuth said. "They go from the vapor to the solid. We're looking at the condensation of very tiny grains and how they grow from the vapor."

To do that, Nuth says his team had to launch a tiny laboratory into the atmosphere. Inside, it heated a filament of silicate dust to around 2,000 degrees Celsius, turning it into gas. It has to be inside a rocket to experience microgravity, allowing scientists to study how cosmic dust particles form and grow without being disturbed.

"If the growth process is efficient, what you make are the equivalent of big rocks," Nuth said. "They're only a tenth of a micron in size, but in astrophysical terms, they're the equivalent of big rocks. If, on the other hand, the growth is very inefficient, they don't grow very much. What you end up with is the equivalent of dust bunnies."

Nuth said the study could further scientific understanding of the dust produced by volcanic eruptions and coal-fired power plants — of which Alaska has both.

Nuth and Fleming said both experiments at the Poker Flat Research Range were successful, and that they were able to recover most of the expensive rocket parts after the launches.

#36. Extensive and expensive, Cooper Landing highway project reaches milestones on long road to completion

COOPER LANDING — A massive road project screeches and rumbles at the center of the Kenai Peninsula this fall, inching toward completion that’s still years away. But the target is in sight for the crew building the new road, which will eventually tie into the existing Sterling Highway.

In September, an excavator clawed at heaps left by a rock blasting crew to load a line of behemoth trucks, which briskly hauled them off. When a load was dumped miles away, the dust hadn’t settled before a dozer pushed them into a layer that will become the road’s subsurface level hundreds of feet above town.

Drill, blast, scoop, haul, dump, crush, roll. A road crew of about 40 is slowly moving mountains. In a few years, it will result in 15 miles of rerouted and reimagined highway corridor to bypass Cooper Landing. The project is transforming the landscape, amassing archaeological data and stirring talk of its potential to change daily life for residents and visitors here. Leaders and engineers say tasks of this magnitude don’t come around often in Alaska.

“I’ll probably never see anything like this in my lifetime,” said Bryan Gallagher, project superintendent for QAP, the general contractor undertaking the roadway portion. A typical big road project might include less than a million cubic yards of material to remove, Gallagher said. This one will likely total close to 6.5 million cubic yards by the time it’s complete.

Near the center of the Cooper Landing bypass is its showstopper: a bridge over Juneau Creek for which engineers are still sorting through superlatives. In September, a project engineer dropped a measuring tape from the bridge down to the creek. At 291 feet, he confirmed it will be the highest road bridge in the state. A railroad bridge elsewhere is higher, but only barely so.

How the bridge was placed is also noteworthy. Traylor Brothers Inc., the other contractor in the joint venture, constructed the bridge on the ground next to the canyon, then pushed, or “launched,” it into position over the course of eight painstaking days in summer. At 928 feet, it’s the longest launched bridge in the U.S., according to Jonathan Tymick, project manager for the state Department of Transportation & Public Facilities.

The Cooper Landing Bypass amounts to something Tymick called “generational.” “It’s definitely one of the largest and most complex projects that DOT’s done,” he said.

Work began in May 2021. When it fully opens — sometime in 2030 is the best estimate — the new stretch of Sterling Highway will route traffic to the north of Cooper Landing across its hillsides. The bypass will climb about 730 feet from the east and 830 from the west. For comparison, the Seward Highway climb from Turnagain Arm to Turnagain Pass is about 900 feet.

The project will include a pedestrian pathway, eight wildlife crossings, improved water drainage channels and a rebuilt parking lot at the Resurrection Pass Trailhead. Several miles of existing highway at both ends of the project have already been improved.

Once in use, most Sterling Highway traffic will bypass the town of about 344 people near where Kenai Lake feeds Kenai River. The Sterling Highway is the only major east-west roadway on the peninsula and connects Homer, Soldotna and Kenai with Anchorage.

Shaun Combs, project engineer for DOT&PF, said studies began examining ways to improve the highway at Cooper Landing more than 40 years ago to address congestion. The traffic problems are most acute in summer, when the Russian and Kenai rivers’ sportfishing renown adds to the population of Southcentral Alaska tourists and resident commuters.

The existing narrow, winding highway — which was originally constructed in the 1940s and 1950s, and crosses the Kenai River twice — has insufficient room for drivers to pull over or pass and is too close to the river, project leaders say. “One of the biggest nightmare scenarios would be to get a double tanker into the Kenai River,” Combs said.

Lines of sight are limited. On the new highway, motorists won’t feel the tension of stopping the flow of traffic to turn. “Instead of 200 feet (of visibility) out here, it’s going to be 1,200 feet, if not a lot more than that,” Combs said. “You’re getting rid of driver frustration. Now you’ve got passing lanes going uphill.”

A 2018 crash analysis by the Federal Highway Administration and Alaska’s DOT&PF noted 303 crashes between Sterling Highway Miles 45 and 60 from 2000 and 2009. Five of those crashes were fatal. The analysis concluded that the Juneau Creek alternative would reduce crashes by approximately 70%.

“Our primary purpose was to eliminate the amount of crashes we’re seeing,” said Tymick.

Combs said finalizing a plan was a yearslong process that involved land purchases, a land swap that required an act of Congress, and the cooperation of several entities, including the Kenai Peninsula Borough, the Kenaitze Indian Tribe, the U.S. Forest Service and Alaska’s Department of Natural Resources.

After years of environmental impact studying and public input, the “Juneau Creek alternative” was formally selected among several options in May 2018, because it “provides the best balance between meeting the project needs and minimizing impacts to the human and natural environment,” according to the state’s news release at the time.

Prior to that selection, the Kenaitze Indian Tribe opposed some options under consideration, said its operations director Joel Isaak. “The route that was ultimately chosen is one the tribe was advocating for, because it eliminates going through the densest areas of cultural impact,” he said.

The bill matches the project’s ambitious scope.

This month, Tymick said the project will likely total $700 million for construction and design. That figure includes the needed land acquisitions and an ongoing archaeological program. Tymick said Alaska will bear about 9% of the cost of the project. The rest is paid through the Highway Trust Fund, the federal mechanism for major road construction funding.

After the route was selected in 2018, news reports pegged the cost at about $375 million. Eight years later, the state’s most recent fact sheet estimates an $832.6 million total. Tymick said the latter figure represents an overestimation that accounts for funding uncertainty that has the potential to extend the timeline, which would increase costs. Still, the figure he says is more realistic is far steeper than early estimates.

“It had a lot of things that I think would’ve been hard for someone to anticipate,” Tymick said of the project.

The cost of the bridge, once estimated to be about $40 million, will end up costing about $160 million, Tymick said. During early estimation, it wasn’t known that shallow groundwater tables would require complicated adjustments, he said. The bridge also needed to be built higher than ground level to accommodate trails under each abutment.

Tymick said costs are evaluated on an ongoing basis, and adjustments have once included a switch to a general contractor with a more competitive bid. “That makes me feel a lot better that we’re being stewards of the federal funding,” he said.

DOT&PF project engineer Marcus Forkner, standing near the east buttress in late summer, said coming this far has been complicated. “Everything with this project has been a logistical challenge, because everything we need has to come from somewhere. A lot of it isn’t available in Alaska,” he said.

Forty-five girders, each 90 to 120 feet in length and about 13 feet tall, were fabricated in Arizona, trucked to California, barged to Seward and hauled to the job site. Tymick said shipping the girders alone cost $9 million. In total, the Juneau Creek bridge comprises nearly 7 million pounds of structural steel and 4,700 cubic yards of concrete.

The 2018 state and federal Record of Decision committed to building the bridge without access to the canyon “to protect Juneau Creek from temporary or permanent fill or channel realignment.” The bridge construction on the nearby ground decreased hazards and eased inspection, said Bridget Monahan, project manager for Traylor Brothers.

The structure first crossed the canyon in June at the conclusion of a launch that took eight days. Four rams pushed the bridge in 10-foot increments on 300- to 400-ton rollers, a task that required watchfulness from surveyors, design engineers, crane operators, support personnel and more. “Seven million pounds of steel to keep an eye on,” Monahan said

“It was very quiet on the last couple pushes,” she said. “The last day was a very big sigh of relief. There was lots of cheering.”

Remaining work for the bridge crew of about 30 includes pouring the concrete bridge deck, backfilling the abutments and adding railings along its pedestrian pathway.

“Right now, we are looking very good to be ahead of schedule,” Monahan said in late summer. “But the Alaska winter could change that at any point.”

While heavy equipment shaped the towering rock at the project’s east end, another team meticulously worked by hand in the soil to the west. In September, Aurora Whitesell, a cultural resources specialist for environmental contractor HDR, took measurements inside a cleanly cut channel of earth. It was part of the group’s effort to search for fire-affected rocks.

“We’re trying to find a thermal feature, something that can give us a date,” she said.

The area is one of many archaeological dig sites that have been or are being scoured for data before the highway bypass project eventually alters the area irrevocably.

The National Historic Preservation Act requires federally funded projects to consider the effects on places of historic significance. The bypass-adjacent program is being carried out through an agreement between the Alaska Office of History and Archaeology, the U.S. Forest Service, the Kenaitze Indian Tribe, CIRI and others, Sweeney said.

“We now have, I would say, a good partnership with other signatories, and that took time to develop,” said Isaak, the Kenaitze Tribe’s operations director.

The Sqilantnu Archaeological District, which uses the Dena’ina name for the area, contains evidence of at least five prehistoric cultures, according to the U.S. Forest Service. Some are located at the west end of the bypass.

“You might think we know the cultural history here, but we don’t,” Sweeney said.

Isaak said the archaeological effort will feed the Kenaitze Tribe’s ongoing project to study salmon DNA in the area. It has also been exciting to uncover evidence of toolmaking in a region that was historically important for hunting and fishing for Indigenous people, he said. In his view, the ongoing work brings Western science in line with information traditionally shared in other ways.

“Our oral histories are very accurate for what’s out there, but that often is not considered to be the same authority as a carbon date,” Isaak said.

In September, Elliot Hubbard, an HDR cultural resource specialist, said hundreds of microblades have been recovered at the location, leading researchers to consider the area a hub for their making. Microblades are razor-sharp blades made of stone, often set in bone or antler, and used in tools like knives and drills.

“We’ve found hundreds of blades at this point,” Hubbard said as the team worked in ditches around him. “But the question is like … where is the material coming from and how are we going to get this radiocarbon dated? Like, how old is this site?”

Meanwhile at a second dig site, Sean Teeter, principal investigator and field director for HDR, said the team had removed the remnants of a prehistoric sweat bath. Fire-cracked rock found there told a unique story, he said.

“We haven’t seen them described this way in ethnographic literature, but its purpose seemed fairly clear from what we found in the structure,” he said.

The tribe is also working to relocate ancestral remains, Isaak said. “We’re working to find an appropriate resting spot for those,” he said.

Sweeney said the archaeology project may conclude in 2027. A final report will follow years later. State cultural resources specialist Erik Hilsinger called the effort one of the largest archaeological undertakings in Alaska history, considering its findings and the number of archaeologists involved.

“This is really significant for a lot of people in the broader context of the history of the state of Alaska,” he said. “I think we got a lot of payoff.”

Isaak said he hopes the legacy of the Kenaitze Tribe’s involvement will include public-facing information and educational material, including a website to be published after the final report is complete. The tribe’s connection to the area never stopped, he said, but the project can improve its visibility.

“One of the goals is to present the information in kind of a first-time-ever from the tribe’s perspective,” he said. “And it will include the Western science data as well, but it’s really from the perspective of the people who have been living here for thousands of years.”

Combs said the public pushback he has encountered on the bypass project mostly happened in its design phase years ago. Driving from the work site back to his office trailer in August, he acknowledged that some businesses may be affected by the new flow of traffic once work is done. But many other Cooper Landing enterprises, like fishing guide services and lodging options, thrive because the river is central to a traveler’s plans.

“We still hear from individuals that just don’t like the idea of the project itself. It doesn’t matter what you do or say, you can never convince that person,” he said. “There’s validity in that. I get it.”

Some people in Cooper Landing are seeing an upside. David Gaines — owner of Kenai Cache Outfitters, which offers fish guiding and processing — said he might notice a small but insignificant business impact. More obvious, he said, would be a quality-of-life improvement “if we can get the big semis off of here,” he said, referring to the highway outside his storefront door.

“The fishermen are going to be here,” he said. “They love the Russian River. They love the Kenai River.”

Down the road, Rachel Mirth carved a log into a bear figure with a chain saw. She commonly sells them to drive-by customers, she said, but she doesn’t feel threatened by the eventual bypass diversion. People intending to enjoy what Cooper Landing offers will still come, she said.

“If there are people who are just intending on flying through and they’re not intending on stopping, go ahead and put them on the bypass,” she said.

Lorraine Temple, a lodging business owner and longtime year-round resident of Cooper Landing who now splits her time in Homer, said she has warmed to the project over the years. “At first I was totally, 100% against it,” Temple said. “Jump forward to today — I think I’m in good company with this — we just want to see it done.”

Temple said she has begun to imagine a future for Cooper Landing that’s more walkable. That could draw visitors, she said: “It could actually become more of a destination.”

Temple said she would have preferred that a route south of the river were chosen for the bypass. But she allowed that the one being built will have benefits.

“Ultimately, I think it’s going to be one of the most beautiful drives in Alaska,” she said.

ANCHORAGE, Alaska — Today, the Bureau of Land Management established a categorical exclusion to improve predictability and reduce burdens for companies seeking permits for wintertime oil and gas exploration in the National Petroleum Reserve in Alaska. This action enables important winter exploration projects—such as exploratory seismic surveys, exploration wells, and plugging and abandonment— to move forward efficiently, advancing American Energy Dominance and national security.

The BLM developed this categorical exclusion after analyzing the dozens of previous environmental assessments the agency has conducted for wintertime exploration in the Petroleum Reserve. Those analyses show that those exploration activities result in no significant impacts on human health or the environment when conducted under established operating procedures and mitigation measures. Wintertime exploration relies on temporary ice roads, ice pads, and snow routes where the only potential sign of exploration after the snow melts is generally the well-head, if drilled.

“The BLM has decades of experience analyzing the effects of exploration in the Petroleum Reserve and permitting responsible exploration,” said BLM Alaska State Director Kevin Pendergast. “This categorical exclusion builds on that record to allow developers to more nimbly locate energy resources in the Petroleum Reserve, ultimately increasing domestic energy supply, leading to stable energy costs and more revenue for Alaskan communities and the American public.”

Categorical exclusions are categories of actions that have been determined to not typically result in significant environmental impacts, eliminating the need conduct a full environmental review. Streamlining National Environmental Policy Act compliance through categorical exclusions removes regulatory burdens and speeds up the process, saving time, and resources.

Before relying on a categorical exclusion for a proposed project, an agency must verify that there are no extraordinary circumstances, such as potential impacts to endangered species or cultural resources, that may result in the project having any significant environmental effects.

This new categorical exclusion supports the goals of Executive Order 14153, “Unleashing Alaska’s Extraordinary Resource Potential,” Executive Order 14154, “Unleashing American Energy,” and the Working Families Tax Cuts Act. The action builds on the BLM’s extensive work to improve regulatory certainty in the Petroleum Reserve, including rescission of the 2024 rule that restricted leasing and development in the Petroleum Reserve and reopening nearly 82% of the area to oil and gas leasing through an updated Integrated Activity Plan. #39. Kenai Peninsula Borough Assembly enacts weather modification permit process

October 23, 2026 by Ashlyn O'Hara, KDLL - Kenai

The Kenai Peninsula Borough now has a way to permit cloud-seeding and other types of geoengineering. That’s after borough assembly members Tuesday enacted a section of code that requires detailed applications from anyone seeking to modify weather on borough land.

The new rules require would-be weather modifiers to obtain a commercial lease or commercial land use permit to use borough land. Among other things, that application would require a description of what type and quantity of chemicals and flares would be used and the frequency and duration of weather modification flights.

Before closing out a land lease with the borough, the group engaging in approved weather modification activities on borough land would need to submit a summary of how they complied with borough code.] And the borough now also defines geoengineering and weather modification as the “deliberate intervention in the earth’s climate system or atmosphere.”

Assembly approval of the new application process comes about two months after a private company used cloud seeding technology to make rain over the southern Kenai Peninsula. The operation, carried out by the California-based Rainmaker Technology Corporation, created roughly one-hundredth of an inch of precipitation over the project area.

Rainmaker’s cloud-seeding work included using drones to disperse silver iodide in clouds. The iodide helps catalyze ice crystals until the crystals are big enough to fall to the ground as precipitation. The company says it tested in Homer due to high cloud coverage, and envisions deploying the technology in drier areas to help fight water scarcity.

When the company celebrated the results of its operation, details of the full project scope came as a surprise to state and local officials. Borough Mayor Peter Micciche in particular says his administration was misled about what exactly the company planned to do in the skies over Homer. He sponsored the ordinance passed Tuesday with assembly member Lenora Niesen.

“Our ability to limit weather modification and geoengineering is limited to borough lands at this point,” he said. “We know that the FAA controls the skies, and NOAA is the only other one that is related to any sort of – actually, NOAA isn’t even approval, it’s just information.”

Assembly members also added a requirement that applicants describe the intended outcome of weather modification activities. The new section of code also bans geoengineering outright on borough land designated for casual use, like berry-picking or skiing.

Rainmaker CEO Augustus Doricko says it received all applicable permits and permissions for its cloud-seeding operation. But he agrees the company could have done more front-end public outreach. Alaska’s Department of Natural Resources says it did not – and could not – give the company permission for cloud-seeding because that activity is not addressed in state law.

Last month, the assembly formally asked state lawmakers to ban all forms of weather modification in Alaska, or, at least regulate it. Homer Republican Rep. Sarah Vance has been a vocal critic of Rainmaker’s Kenai Peninsula operation. If re-elected, she says she’ll sponsor a bill establishing clear guidelines for weather modification.

As assembly members in a second-class borough, the local government is limited in what policies it can enact on the issue. The new section of code is effective immediately.

#42. Alaska Lt. Gov. incorrectly put disqualified candidate on the ballot, state judge rules

The Alaska Division of Elections and Lt. Gov. Nancy Dahlstrom acted incorrectly when they put disqualified Republican candidate Jose Tagle on the ballot for state House District 22, an Anchorage Superior Court judge said Thursday.

Judge Josie Garton’s decision, published in the late afternoon, means “Tagle’s election may not be certified if he does win a majority of votes in the general election,” she wrote.

Tagle is running against Democratic incumbent Rep. Ted Eischeid, who is heavily favored in the district that went for Democratic Presidential candidate Kamala Harris two years ago.

Garton did not order the division to reprint ballots without Tagle’s name. Absentee and early voting is already taking place, and Tagle may appeal the ruling. A successful appeal could see his candidacy restored.

“The Division’s certification, while erroneous, does not undermine the fundamental fairness of the election,” Garton said.

The Alaska Division of Elections, Alaska Department of Law and Dahlstrom’s office did not immediately respond to a request for their opinion of the decision.

Tagle did not immediately respond to an email on Thursday afternoon seeking his thoughts.

The head of the Alaska Democratic Party, which filed the lawsuit that challenged Tagle’s candidacy, said she was pleased with the decision.

“Mr. Tagle’s completely blank disclosure left Alaskans in the dark,” said party executive director Jenny-Marie Stryker. “Voters deserve to know the financial interests of our elected officials — that’s the law, and it’s just what’s right.”

Thursday’s ruling comes after a two-month-old dispute that also involved Republican gubernatorial candidate Treg Taylor.

The Alaska Public Offices Commission, which regulates candidates and campaigns, recommended in late August that Tagle be disqualified as a candidate after submitting a blank financial disclosure form and failing to amend it within a grace period allowed by state law.

It also recommended Taylor be disqualified for filing an incomplete form and failing to amend it before the deadline. Taylor and Tagle submitted amended forms after the deadline.

Dahlstrom initially followed APOC’s recommendation, but days later, she reversed herself and put the two men back on the general election ballot, citing a review from the Department of Law indicating that disclosure forms filed by many other candidates could also have problems.

The Beacon analyzed that review and found many of the problems flagged by the Department were either minor or did not exist at all.

The Alaska Democratic Party sued the Division of Elections in July after it permitted Tagle’s candidacy for the state primary election despite his blank financial disclosure form.

It did not challenge Taylor’s candidacy, and subsequent proceedings did not involve him or Dahlstrom’s decision to certify him.

Garton ruled against the party in August, saying that under state law, Tagle had 30 days to fix his blank form, and the party had filed before that grace period was over. In addition, Garton said that the commission, not the Division of Elections, should have the final word on the correctness of financial disclosure forms.

After Dahlstrom certified Tagle despite the APOC recommendation, the Democratic Party filed a new complaint in September and asked Garton to re-examine the issue. She declined to issue an immediate ruling and held oral arguments Oct. 9.

During oral arguments, attorneys from the Department of Law — representing Dahlstrom and the Division of Elections — abandoned the earlier argument that Tagle’s restoration was warranted by the fact that other candidates also had problematic forms.

Instead, they contended that APOC’s disqualification recommendation was not final and that procedural errors — including a notice that failed to state that Tagle could appeal the recommendation — meant that Dahlstrom was not bound by the recommendation.

But writing on Thursday, Garton concluded that based on state laws, regulations and the procedures used by APOC, the commission’s decision was “final and appealable.”

She wrote that while APOC’s statement was officially billed as a recommendation, state law requires the lieutenant governor to follow it.

“While it may be true that APOC’s recommendation ‘did not order anyone to do anything,’ both parties agree, as does the court, that the Lt. Governor has no discretion to disregard a final decision by APOC that Tagle failed to substantially comply with (state law) within the deadline,” Garton wrote.

State attorneys argued that APOC’s recommendation didn’t take effect until 30 days after it was ordered and that procedural problems also left the door open for the lieutenant governor not to follow the recommendation, but Garton disagreed.

“APOC’s decision was effective immediately, and the potential procedural irregularities in APOC’s proceedings did not provide grounds for the lieutenant governor to ignore the clear command of (state law),” she wrote.

Garton said the issues facing Tagle’s nomination “are precisely the circumstances under which the Legislature intended a candidate for public office to forfeit his nomination.”

“By certifying Tagle’s nomination after APOC entered a final decision finding that Tagle failed to substantially comply … within the deadline established by law, the Lieutenant Governor and the Director of the Division of Elections violated (state law).”

Garton concluded that APOC should notify Tagle that he has 30 days to administratively challenge the disqualification decision. He — or state officials — could also appeal Garton’s ruling to the Alaska Supreme Court.

#50. Food Bank of Alaska is expanding to better prepare for disasters

The Food Bank of Alaska is expanding its space in Anchorage to better prepare for statewide disasters.

Food Bank officials on Tuesday gave a tour of the new 34,000-square-foot building, which is right next to their current facility near downtown. They also announced a $150,000 grant from the KeyBank Foundation.

The food bank will use the building and money for disaster preparedness, CEO Cara Durr said.

She said that the food bank has seen a sustained increase of need for resources over the past few years.

“We're doing all we can just to get food out the door to our partners and to our neighbors in need,” Durr said. “So it's really important for us to be able to have some dedicated space and funding to prepare for those larger disasters.”

The food bank bought the new building a few years ago. It used a $4 million state grant to purchase the building for $3 million, with the rest to be used for renovations.

Durr said the KeyBank grant will go toward shelf-stable food and transportation to help deploy resources more quickly during emergencies.

In a presentation Tuesday, Daniel Bentle with the food bank said that by the time their resources from the Lower 48 reached Western Alaska during ex-Typhoon Halong, victims had already been evacuated to Anchorage.

Durr said that the disaster preparedness initiative will help the food bank stock inventory ahead of disasters, allowing them to provide assistance to communities faster.

The food bank, working with partners, distributes resources across the state and often gets three to four requests for help each week, Durr said. But they don’t have dedicated funding to address those requests, she said. And with rising fuel and food costs, the funds the food bank does have aren’t able to stretch as far.

“Rising fuel and energy costs have had a huge impact, not only from the perspective of our neighbors, but you know we rely on so many different modes of transportation, which use expensive diesel,” Durr said. “Those costs have all gone up.”

The food bank received $1.6 million from the state to continue an emergency food assistance program that sends food boxes to partners across the state.

The emergency preparedness building will be completed by the summer.
